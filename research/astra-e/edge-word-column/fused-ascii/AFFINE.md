# Can a certificate remove the second-queue gathers?

For a physical target subinterval of a literal partner band, T(y)=Q[k+y−d]. Q is strictly increasing within that byte queue. The sequence Q[i]−i is therefore nondecreasing. Matching displacement at the first and last observed target rows is necessary and sufficient for a constant translation on those observed rows, and then certifies every intervening row as well. Loading the two endpoints once gives the full formula on success. A singleton already costs only one lookup; a two-row certificate consumes both values it might have saved.

On the 187 packable admissions the direct constructor performs 13,429,533 second-Q gathers. Although 68,665 of 371,304 original ASCII bands are wholly affine, those bands cover only 190,404 needed gathers (1.42%). For the actual coalesced directory, only 24,057 of 188,814 ASCII ranges are affine, covering 60,090 needed gathers (0.45%). The gap between band count and visit mass is important. The frozen block counts are 907 of 67,222 across original bands and 205 across merged ranges.

A stronger certificate looks separately at the minimum and maximum encountered *eligible* y of every source claim in a partner range, omitting a possible interior primary. It grants discovery of the end of a claim for free and reuses both tested values in the output even on failure. Across 1,611,822 nonempty-or-primary-only ASCII-to-ASCII range claims, 668,957 have one displacement across the actually used rows. After paying their endpoint reads, only 878,285 extra Q loads can be avoided (6.54%). Splitting the target directory at every displacement change, and merging whenever byte and actual displacement agree, eliminates all gathers at the price of raising 254,003 physical descriptors to 8,851,997, and 1,769,715 partner-range claims to 8,998,594. The width-independent direct publisher had already lost its construction benchmark by 35% on these blocks. The arithmetic certificate offers neither broad cheap coverage nor a small fully refined directory.

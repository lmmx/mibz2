## Where the constructions meet

**Astra E recovers the parse-BWT ordering that cyclic prefix-free parsing uses as input.** Boucher, Gagie, Kuhnle, Langmead, Manzini and Mun (2019) parse the padded `#T$` with `w` copies of the terminal padding. A dictionary phrase starts at one length-`w` trigger and ends at the end of the next. Consecutive phrases share the trigger window. The parse lists dictionary IDs in text order and names them by lexicographic phrase rank. The dictionary suffixes of length greater than `w`, including full phrases, separate the byte suffixes into ordered blocks (Lemmas 1–6). For equal dictionary suffixes, Lemma 7 transfers order from the parse suffix beginning at the shared ending trigger. Big-BWT uses the inverted lists of `BWT(P)` and the dictionary to emit every block of `BWT(T$)`. Every emitted entry is a preceding **byte**. [Boucher et al., 2019, theory and implementation](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13015-019-0148-5)

The cyclic version is the closer match. Boucher, Cenzato, Lipták, Rossi and Sciortino (SPIRE 2021, §4) parse circular inputs having a trigger in each input string. Lemma 16 and Proposition 17 give the order of text rotations by dictionary suffix and then by the rotation order of the remaining parse. The proof of Proposition 17 explicitly compares rotations **starting at triggers** with cyclic parse rotations. For a single circular text, select the text rows beginning at any trigger. Their relative order is the order of the corresponding parse rotations, so assigning each row its preceding full phrase ID gives the parse last column. The ordinary text last column at those ranks stores one predecessor byte. Recovering the phrase labels from that column is the work of Astra's first-return constructor. [Cyclic PFP, §4, Lemma 16 and Proposition 17](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.11191#page=12)

| Case | Exact correspondence and conditions |
| --- | --- |
| F2 with one delimiter byte `d` | Set the cyclic trigger set to `{d}` and `w=1`. The F2 section is the rows immediately after `d`, with a phrase `u` ending at the next `d`. The corresponding PFP phrase is `d u`. Starting at `d` and advancing one byte preserves the relative order within its bucket. Prepending the common byte to the dictionary preserves its order. Thus the two parse last columns coincide after renaming, with stable order for equal rows. |
| Several triggers or wider windows | Cyclic PFP orders rows at **trigger starts** by the full overlapping phrases. F2 with a delimiter set uses **after-delimiter** rows and right-closed tokens. Advancing is order-preserving within each trigger cylinder, but it can reorder rows across distinct triggers. The singleton identification does not license using the after-delimiter ordering for a union. For a fixed compound marker `Q`, the trigger-start version and F2-word use the same section, with an overlapping PFP phrase `XQ` for a first-return spelling `X`. Keeping the terminal context in the collation matters if one spelling is a prefix of another. |
| Marks and repetition | The 2019 linear construction recovers the terminated text's BWT with its sentinel convention. Cyclic PFP's eBWT represents circular inputs, with the start-of-text positions tracked separately when wanted. Astra carries a primary row, its byte offset in a return and the original emission length. The latter two let it emit the requested length from a short period. Its fallback handles a marked cycle without a trigger. The PFP theorem assumes coverage and gives no such fallback. |

[Astra F2](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/2b19f2e2f3430b9efe23467de968959c6059a272/research/astra-e/formulations/f2/README.md) · [Astra F2-word](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/2b19f2e2f3430b9efe23467de968959c6059a272/research/astra-e/formulations/f2-word/README.md) · [Cyclic PFP, §§2 and 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.11191)

The direction reverses at the level of the representation. Big-BWT obtains the labels by scanning raw text, sorts the parse, and uses that result to assemble all original byte blocks. F2 starts with the run-length byte transform and discovers return labels and their order through LF and stable selection. Given the cyclic dictionary and parse transform, a forward cyclic construction can reconstruct the byte transform on the covered input under compatible conventions. That representation-level relationship gives no inverse construction algorithm or cost bound from the PFP paper.

## Prior art for the bounds and the compound marker

| Astra claim | Closest published result and its scope |
| --- | --- |
| `t ≤ r` for one marker and `t ≤ r+k−1` for `k` distinct, equally long markers | Here `r` counts maximal input last-column runs and `t` counts runs of complete return labels at the **marker starts** in rank order. I found neither exact finite labelled bound stated in the papers checked. Dolce and Perrin (2017), Proposition 3.16 and Theorems 3.17 and 3.19, prove that word cylinders in a regular IET are admissible and derive a regular exchange with the same number of intervals, with its partition coded by return words. Berk, Trujillo and Wu (2025), §2.2, trace arbitrary single-interval induced breakpoints to original breakpoints and two endpoints, giving at most `d+2` intervals. This supplies substantial precedent for the charging mechanism. The regularity hypothesis and counts of coding or translation intervals leave Astra's exact counts for finite, labelled LF sections, including disconnected cycles and unions, open as a citation. [Dolce–Perrin](https://www-igm.univ-mlv.fr/~perrin/Recherche/Publications/Articles/ita170002.pdf) · [Berk–Trujillo–Wu, §2.2](https://jep.centre-mersenne.org/item/10.5802/jep.302.pdf) |
| Counts under parsing versus counts under morphisms | Ferenczi and Zamboni, Theorem 6 and Corollary 7, treat concatenations of return words and identify the pair of induced orders under their clustering/order-condition hypothesis. Dolce and Hughes, Theorem 1, show that a single return word from the language of a regular IET clusters under its own BWT. These are close precedents in the clustering setting. Fici et al. (MFCS 2025), §§4–6, analyze the change in run count when expanding by a fixed morphism, with main results for binary alphabets. The direction, assumptions and counted objects differ from a fixed marker section of a general input BWT. [Ferenczi–Zamboni](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.17370) · [Dolce–Hughes](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.18280) · [Fici et al.](https://doi.org/10.4230/LIPIcs.MFCS.2025.49) |
| F2-word's two stages collapse to one compound marker | **The return-word identity has a direct precedent.** Durand (1998), Proposition 6(5), derives first on `u` and then on a word `v` of derived symbols. One derivation on the compound word `Θ_u(v)u` gives the same derived sequence in the return-word naming convention. Take `u=d` and `v` one token ID. Durand cuts on the marker's **start**, so if `a=Θ_d(v)` and Astra's corresponding right-closed token is `w`, then `a d = d w`. His compound word is therefore Astra's `Q=d w`. This covers the sequence-level collapse on a covered periodic orbit. His naming is by first appearance. The BWT-specific consequence is the identification of finite rank successors and token runs under the rank-preserving shift `Ψ^|Q|`, with the phase change `XQ=QY` for the two spellings, including overlaps. I found that explicit rank/phase formulation in the Astra record. [Durand, §2.3, Proposition 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0807.3322#page=5) · [Astra F2-word](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/2b19f2e2f3430b9efe23467de968959c6059a272/research/astra-e/formulations/f2-word/README.md) |

The PFP and r-index papers give different guarantees. Gagie, Navarro and Prezza's r-index theorem counts runs of the **indexed text**, and Kuhnle et al. (2020), §3.1, augment the forward PFP emission with suffix-array sampling at those byte-run boundaries. The analogous eBWT index also measures runs of the indexed collection. Kempa and Kociumaka's compressed synchronizing sets, §V, bound representations of samples and relate the original BWT run count to LZ77. None of these statements supplies the two exact constants for a token last column. The literature supports describing those constants as a finite labelled-BWT result for this construction, with the qualification that this search found no exact prior statement. The central compound-marker idea should be attributed to classical return-word derivation. [Gagie–Navarro–Prezza, Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.10382) · [Kuhnle et al., §3.1](https://doi.org/10.1089/cmb.2019.0309) · [Boucher et al., extended r-index](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ic.2024.105155) · [Kempa–Kociumaka, §V](https://www3.cs.stonybrook.edu/~kempa/files/FOCS2020.pdf) · [Astra F2-bounds](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/2b19f2e2f3430b9efe23467de968959c6059a272/research/astra-e/formulations/f2-bounds/README.md)

## What transfers to the first-level budget

Boucher et al.'s Theorem 1 gives `O(n)` time and workspace proportional to `||D|| + |P|` for RAM construction given the dictionary and parse. The hash predicate controls cut rate empirically. The paper's Table 3 explains how a long homogeneous stretch either supplies repeated triggers or lies inside a long phrase, depending on the window hash. Figure 5 finds different parameter pairs for minimum memory and minimum time on the same input. Treating hashes as uniform is a density heuristic for choosing a benchmark, with overlapping and repeated windows affecting spacing and tails. Oliva et al. (DCC 2022, §3 and Theorem 3.2) give an explicit byte objective `||D|| + W|P|`, with `W` bytes per phrase ID, and a trigger-deletion algorithm using already parsed phrase-pair frequencies. Oliva, Gagie and Boucher (DCC 2023) reparse the *available* first parse to reduce its memory burden while constructing the text BWT. That establishes prior art for parsing a parse. F2c induces directly from the first run column and displaces the first dense link table, so that construction has a different input and work profile. [Original PFP, Theorem 1, Table 3 and Figure 5](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13015-019-0148-5) · [Trigger filtering, §3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11135878/) · [Recursive PFP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11328891/) · [F2c](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/2b19f2e2f3430b9efe23467de968959c6059a272/research/astra-e/formulations/f2c/README.md)

For Astra, the transferable terms are retained rows `m`, spelled dictionary bytes `B`, and their representation-dependent storage charges. For a one-byte marker `m` is known from the front histogram. For a fixed word it is the FM interval width after the search. A dense phase may pay a stride times `m`. A run-encoded phase writes `t` records, with a capacity bound from F2-bounds. These make useful output and memory terms. The frozen F2 example had 135,716 retained rows, 6,133 output runs and 83,454 dictionary bytes, alongside 73,276 first-run searches and 18,994 merge pieces. The latter counts record work the PFP byte objective does not measure. The paid portfolio also tested quote density as a selector for two inversion arms and got a held-out ratio of 1.0052 to fixed POPCNT on 93 blocks. That is one test of density as an arm selector, with the scope of the archived local CPU data. [F2 charged counters](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/2b19f2e2f3430b9efe23467de968959c6059a272/research/astra-e/formulations/f2/README.md) · [Paid portfolio](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/2b19f2e2f3430b9efe23467de968959c6059a272/research/astra-e/budget/portfolio/README.md)

The corresponding first-level time budget needs terms for actual rank and LF discovery, selector and merge work, dictionary/link construction, and peak live state. The rank-tape replay is the closest published control within the project. On nine admissions, prepaid interval and label answers reduced first discovery from 7.149 to 1.936 ms and whole inverse from 16.474 to 11.189 ms by the sum of per-block medians, retaining tape read and publication costs. The broader 228-admission model leaves about `0.0532×` admitted Lazy inverse CPU for answer discovery **if** the oracle's `0.675×` inverse ratio generalizes, scanner and copy are included in the local additive denominator, and other costs hold. The nine measured blocks account for 2.88% of that denominator. These numbers describe different scopes, and the local additive ceiling supplies no i9 wall prediction. PFP offers a storage model and a trigger-selection analogy. The archived paid metrics and oracle define the relevant CPU question for this constructor.

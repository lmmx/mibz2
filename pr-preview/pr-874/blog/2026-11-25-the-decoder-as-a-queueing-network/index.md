# The decoder as a queueing network

Modelled as stations and queues, the decoder shows in advance where more workers stop helping and which experiments to run next.

25 November 2026 · 18 min read · [Research in progress](<#10-what-runs-next>)

mibz2's decoder is one serial scanner feeding parallel workers, and the simplest model of such a thing, a two-station queue, turns out to describe it almost exactly. Measured at one to four workers, it predicts where adding workers stops helping, at about 7.5 on our ten-core machine, and the measurement across every count from 1 to 20 puts the crossover at 7.67. Above that point the scanner sets the speed, so reorganising how work is queued can recover only a few per cent; real gains need less work at the bottleneck, and when they arrive the bottleneck moves back to the workers, so improvements have to be priced together. Reading the performance-analysis literature with several independent readers then caught an error in our own first reading and found about 8% of the scanner's work in two avoidable copies. The experiments that follow say which mechanism controls the decoder at which worker count, and so which costs belong to engineering and which might yield to mathematics; the first of them finds the workers' construction and emission in control below the knee and the scanner's Huffman decode at and above it. This post picks up where [Posing the inversion problem](<https://lmmx.github.io/mibz2/pr-preview/pr-874/blog/2026-11-24-posing-the-inversion-problem/index.md>) left off.

**Takeaways**

- A two-station model fitted on four worker counts predicts the knee at 7.49 workers; the measured crossover is 7.67, and the decoder's best wall, 0.191 s, is at 8.
- Above the knee the decoder runs at 0.945 or better of the busiest server's limit at every count, so no queueing discipline can recover more than about 5%.
- Independent readings corrected the first: the public API never runs 20 workers on this machine, and the scanner spends about 8% of its demand on two avoidable copies.
- Stage 1 of the characterisation confirmed the switch from workers to scanner at exactly 8, and found that asking for 3, 5 or 7 threads selects an older kernel that is 61% slower at 7.
- Stage 2 showed the switch by intervention: at 6 workers construction and emission control the wall, at 8 and 10 only the scanner's Huffman decode does, and the wall behaves as the maximum of the two stations, so a faster scanner gains little at 8 while workers sharing cores cost 48%.
- Two claims were corrected: the price for decoding several symbols a lookup was for a change master already had, and a third symbol gained nothing; and the first held-out prediction passed only on wide intervals.

## 1\. A simple observation

The four-arm test supplied each of the decoder's two stages for free, outside the clock. At ten workers, supplying the codes for emission saved nothing measurable (0.976 of the shipped time), supplying the scanner's output saved 19% (0.806), and supplying both saved 26% (0.741): a gain invisible until another bottleneck was removed ([Masked bottlenecks in a two-stage decoder](<https://lmmx.github.io/mibz2/pr-preview/pr-874/blog/2026-11-24-the-scanner-hides-the-emitter/index.md>)). An observation like that asks for a model of the decoder as a system rather than as a list of kernels, which is what the programme set out in the previous post is for.

## 2\. The first model, and its first test

The simplest model is a serial stage feeding k identical parallel servers. Each block costs the scanner s and a worker w, so the decoder's wall time is set by the workers, as a/k, until enough of them are running that the scanner cannot keep up, after which it is set by the scanner alone. The crossover falls at k = w/s. From the measurements at one to four workers only, s = 106.6 µs and w = 798.3 µs a block, which puts the knee at 7.49 workers. Across all twenty counts the measured crossover is 7.67, and a fit of c + max(b, a/k) gives a = 1.279 s, b = 0.178 s and c = 0.032 s ([journal §57](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>); [the data](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/math-targets/data/measurements-scan-profile.tsv>)).

Wall time against workers: the knee predicted from four counts

Measured on an i9-10900K (10 cores, 20 threads), one corpus of 1,724 blocks and 839 MB of output, median of 20 decodes at each count, unpinned. The fitted model (steel) has the workers' asymptote a/k on the left and the scanner's b on the right; the dashed line is the knee predicted from counts 1 to 4 alone. The wall at one worker, 1.30 s, is off the scale.

The best wall is 0.191 s, at 8 workers (4,390 MB/s of output), and from 8 to 14 workers it stays flat at 0.197 ± 0.005 s. At 16 and above it steps up 20%, to 0.230 s, because the scanner itself slows by 23% while its share of the wall stays at 0.97. The scanner's work is 81% Huffman decoding, at 1.41 ns a symbol at one worker and 1.51 ns from three. The second figure shows the switch from the scanner's side: at one worker it spends most of its time waiting for a free slot, and from 8 workers it almost never waits.

The scanner's time, working and waiting

Measured, same run. Below the knee the scanner waits for the workers to free a slot; from 8 workers on it works without pause, so it is the bottleneck and only a lighter scanner moves the wall.

One question follows directly: could a better queueing discipline, size-aware dispatch, say, or helpers for a slow block, do far better? The operational laws answer it before any code is written. No discipline raises the ceiling, which the demands set; a discipline can only bring the decoder closer to it ([the discipline lead](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/leads/queueing-discipline.md>)). And the decoder already runs at 0.945 to 0.998 of the busiest server's limit at every worker count, so no reordering could recover more than about 5%, or 3% on the plateau. The large gains lie in less work at the bottleneck, or more servers for it.

## 3\. Reading the field, four times over

Three performance-analysis courses were read in full, with their sources saved, and then mined for opportunities against these measurements by four readers, each writing an answer before seeing the others ([the summary](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/reports/2026-11-25-course-mining.md>)). The fourth, aimed at where mathematics rather than engineering fits, is the subject of §5.

The disagreements were the useful part. The first reading had claimed that the Python binding's default of 20 workers costs 20% of the wall. Two readers, independently, found that the public API never runs 20 workers on this machine: a request for ten or more threads builds 7 workers with a 28-slot window, so the live question is 7 against 8, 0.207 s against 0.191 s, about 7.6% for under 1% more CPU ([journal §61](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>)). The cap of 7 was set because workers beyond it bought nothing; it is a symptom of the scanner's limit rather than a reason to stop raising it.

The second agent's reading found engineering that the first had missed, both confirmed in the code ([journal §62](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>)). At the boundary between the feeder's 4 MiB chunks and the scanner's 2 MiB look-ahead, the scanner copies the held part of its window on its own thread, about 10 ms at 8 workers, or 5.4% of its demand. And each block's symbol buffer is zero-filled again when it is reused, about 5 ms more, driven by the 792 tiny blocks in the corpus. Together that is about 15 ms, 8% of the scanner's demand, with no algorithmic risk, and it has been routed for implementation as an ordinary performance change.

The outside reading supplied the warning that prices every such change. A cut in the scanner's work moves the bottleneck back to the workers: at 8 workers, a 5% cut in the scanner's Huffman demand already reaches the workers' limit, so scanner and worker improvements must be priced together, with the worker count retuned. Stage 1's figures then re-priced the copy fix itself: at 8 workers the workers sit within 0.3 ms of the scanner, so a 15 ms cut there is almost entirely masked (0.1941 s against 0.1944 s), while at 10 workers it is worth about 7.5% ([journal §69](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>)). Those figures are instrumented, so a paired run at 8, 9 and 10 workers decides between the two estimates.

## 4\. Stage 1: the decoder across twenty counts

The first stage of the characterisation ran 840 grid decodes across every count from 1 to 20, with every block's route, stage boundaries and queue transitions logged ([journal §66](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>); [the report](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/math-targets/data/character-stage1/report.txt>)). All nine operational-law identities held in every decode, Little's law exactly. What it found:

- **The two-station model holds.** The limit moves from the workers to the scanner at exactly 8 workers, with a bootstrap interval of \[8, 8\], and a best wall of 0.1944 s there.
- **The scanner limits above the knee, by concurrent state.** The workers' idle time while the scanner is working rises from 0.035 of their time at 8 workers to 0.124 at 10 and 0.551 at 20; their idle time while it waits for input stays under 0.01.
- **The reader is not a co-bottleneck:** its demand is 0.017–0.035 ms a block against the scanner's 0.099–0.130.
- **Contention inflates service per visit.** The scanner's Huffman decode costs 84 µs a block at one worker and 105–107 µs at 16 and above; emission rises from 526 µs at one worker to 816 at 14, and the memory controller's reads per decode from 219 MiB to 1,957 at 16, which at about 8 GB/s at 10 workers points to cache capacity rather than bandwidth.
- **The public API selects an older kernel below 8.** Asking for 3, 5 or 7 threads builds the eager kernel, because the lazy plan begins at 8 workers and the measured settings cover only 4, 6 and 10 or more. At 7 threads that is 0.337 s and 140 MB, against 0.209 s and 54 MB for seven lazy workers: 61% slower, with 2.6 times the memory. It is a production default, so it goes to a registered recalibration across corpora.

The run flagged its own instrument as perturbing at 10 and 20 workers: the cost check's intervals include 1 but are wide, so timings from the ledgers at high counts may be inflated by up to about 9%, while shares, counts and the identities are unaffected.

## 5\. The fourth reading, and a design space

The fourth reading registered its plan for analysing stages 1 and 2 before stage 1 ran, and then asked what the measurements imply about the scanner itself ([record 129](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/7f18ccf719a0fd7eb863f8aa519a4fb8a8f2295b/docs/journal/monodromy/2026-11-25-course-mining-and-analysis-plan.md>)). At 1.41 to 1.51 ns a symbol, the Huffman scan takes about 7 cycles a symbol, one first-level cache load's latency: the scan is a single dependent chain running at its latency floor. The lever is therefore fewer lookups a symbol, decoding several symbols with each lookup. A 10-bit window bounds that at about 2.6 symbols a lookup, and the reading conjectures 13 to 17% off the best wall; the cheapest test is to count cycles a symbol on the scanner at one worker, where about 7 cycles and no cache misses would confirm the floor.

A companion record enumerated the ways the scanner's obligations could be divided between the scanner and the workers, and scored each placement with the two-station law ([record 130](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/9c3690f299574ea5296e189c8f3d74e7dcfb19af/docs/journal/monodromy/2026-11-25-design-space-and-identification.md>)). Three placements lead, all conjectured: an index of block starts, so that each worker scans its own block, at −15%; decoding several symbols a lookup, still serially, at −17%; and both together at −19%. Its separating principle is to keep an obligation on a serial path only if its state has no small summary, and to cut the path wherever the format resets that state. One placement it rules out: decoding a single block's Huffman codes in parallel. The table is chosen afresh every 50 symbols by a running symbol count that nothing in the stream re-synchronises, so each chunk would have to carry at least 50 hypotheses, and a k-way split loses for every k below 50; the obstruction is proved from the format, its cost argued.

The same record measured where the workers' time goes on a real block at one worker: construction of the inversion's structures 45%, emission 38%, move-to-front and run records 10%, and the final run-length expansion and checksum 6%, shares that hold at 8 and 16 workers. Construction, not emission, is the largest target for mathematics in the workers. Of the two leads at the scanner, decoding several symbols a lookup became an engineering prototype, and the index of block starts the larger structural lead.

Of a price already paid

The prototype found the fourth reading's price for decoding several symbols a lookup built on a premise that was already out of date ([journal §82](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/9c476fd302741471ea7eedb9b544afaf17c603c0/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>)). They assumed one symbol a lookup, but master's table has held up to two symbols an entry since [\#745](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/pull/745>): on wikidata it takes 1.85 symbols a lookup, 84% of them pairs, so the 13 to 17% was the price of a change already made. The prototype then tried a third symbol a lookup ([\#870](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/pull/870>)). Its output was byte-identical and its lookups fell 12%, but its time did not: the scanner ran 0.98 to 1.01 of master's time a symbol on wikidata, and slower on two other corpora: a lookup already costs about twice its dependency chain, and the third symbol's gain was eaten by mispredictions and spills in the tail paths. It was rejected. What remains at the scanner is its copies, about 2% of its time from counting each batch's bits at once, a core of its own, and the structural answer, a scan split across cores, which removes the single serial core instead of speeding it.

Stage 1's traces have also been analysed by program ([journal §71](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/3ad28015eb83951d79fdb13cedb4b5bc8ba1d992/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>); [the outputs](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/tree/3ad28015eb83951d79fdb13cedb4b5bc8ba1d992/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/math-targets/data/character-stage1/analysis>)). The lower bound, the busiest of the scanner, the workers and the reader, is tight below the knee, with the wall within 1.4% of it up to 6 workers, and loosest at the knee itself, 4 to 5% at 8 to 11 workers, where dependencies, starvation and blocking cost most. Issuing blocks is smooth, but taking them is bursty: when a slow block at the head of the line finishes, a run of ready blocks is taken at once, the signature of putting completed blocks back in order. These describe one corpus's traces, not guarantees.

## 6\. Stage 2: which activity controls the wall

Stage 1 inferred the switch at the knee from busy times; stage 2 tests it by intervention, in two parts: the first unpinned ([journal §74](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/9c476fd302741471ea7eedb9b544afaf17c603c0/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>); [its report](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/9c476fd302741471ea7eedb9b544afaf17c603c0/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/math-targets/data/character-stage2/report.txt>)), the second with the scanner pinned to a core of its own ([journal §78](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/9c476fd302741471ea7eedb9b544afaf17c603c0/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>); [its report](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/9c476fd302741471ea7eedb9b544afaf17c603c0/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/math-targets/data/character-stage2b/report.txt>)). Each activity is slowed by 20% in turn, and the share of that slowing which reaches the wall, its elasticity, says whether it controls the decode at that worker count: near 1 for the bottleneck, near 0 for an activity with slack behind it.

Which activity controls the wall, at 6, 8 and 10 workers

Measured: each activity slowed 20% alone; 6 workers from stage 2a (6 blocks), 8 and 10 from stage 2b with the scanner pinned to its own core (16 blocks). Solid where the report finds an activity controls the wall, pale where it doesn't resolve or is negligible. The scanner's Huffman decode is amber, the workers' activities blue. At 10 workers several worker coefficients fall below 0, so slowing them speeds the wall, which the run reads as contention and leaves unexplained in detail.

- **Below the knee, the workers control it.** At 6 workers the two parts of construction (0.14 and 0.15) and emission (0.37) control the wall, while the scanner's Huffman decode does not (−0.01).
- **At and above the knee, the scanner does.** At 8 and 10 workers only the scanner's Huffman decode controls the wall: 0.53 and 0.74 in the first part, and 0.795 \[0.681, 0.908\] and 0.577 \[0.420, 0.734\] in the pinned second, whose intervals are two to four times narrower. Nothing in the workers resolves. This is stage 1's switch at 8, now shown by intervening rather than inferred.
- **The wall is a maximum, not a sum.** In the first part, the screen's coefficients, composed as a sum of logarithms, predicted an arm kept out of every fit, the Huffman decode and emission both slowed by 10%: 1.053 against 1.052 observed at 8 workers, each inside its interval. An earlier version of this post called that the programme's first validated predictor. The second part's narrower intervals refute it: the sum predicts 1.101 at 8 workers against 1.069 observed, and 1.034 at 10 against 1.063, both outside, and the first part's pass came from its wide intervals. The larger of the two coefficients alone fits all four tests, and the surface at 8 workers, both stations slowed together, has a roundness of 0.500 \[0.454, 0.548\], the value of a sharp maximum. Since that model was chosen after the failure, it is registered as the predictor for the next held-out test, not counted as validated ([§8's distinction](<#8-the-dynamical-layer>)).
- **A faster scanner barely helps at 8.** Slowing the scanner at 8 workers costs 0.7 to 1.0 of each unit of slowing, but speeding it gains only 0.13 to 0.18, because the workers are close behind and the bottleneck moves to them at once. The wall also failed the registered test of scaling evenly with the paced activities, so these figures are descriptive rather than formal.
- **The queue shows which side binds.** Slowing the scanner by half over two hundred blocks empties the job queue at 8 workers, its mean depth falling from 4.4 to 0.3; slowing emission fills it instead, from 3.9 to 8.9. Either way the decoder recovers within 2 to 4 ms.
- **Where threads sit matters more than the gates.** Widening the window or the byte cap changes nothing beyond noise. Putting workers on the two hardware threads of shared cores, rather than on separate cores, costs 48% at 8 workers; a memory stream running alongside slows the wall by 31 to 33%, and a pointer chase by 110 to 113%. Pinning the scanner to a quiet core alone runs at 0.960 of the unpinned wall at 8 workers. With ten cores, 8 workers plus the scanner, reader and feeder already make eleven threads, so some sharing is unavoidable at 8 and certain at 10.

The first part priced the scanner's copy fix optimistically: cutting the Huffman decode's work by 8%, about the copies' share, implies a wall of 0.957 at 8 workers and 0.940 at 10. The second part makes that an upper bound twice over, since a faster scanner gains little at 8 and more workers, where it would gain, share more cores. Every scanner speed-up is therefore priced at the count it will run at, with the worker count retuned, and the paired comparison of the copy fix decides it.

Of noise that came from the clock

Part of the noise in these runs came from the machine, not the decoder ([journal §81](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/9c476fd302741471ea7eedb9b544afaf17c603c0/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>)). The Pathfinder found the processor's clock at two levels, 4.91 GHz and a low level of 4.09 to 4.56 GHz, with about a quarter of decodes caught low. A direct test traced it to the idle pause each recipe takes before a timed decode: after 0.2 s idle a decode runs at 2.96 GHz and takes 45% longer, and even after the standard 1 s pause, 40% of decodes run low. Spinning every CPU for 100 ms before each timed decode removes the low state and cuts the spread of the log wall from 4.4% to 1.1%, so a test needs about sixteen times fewer rounds for the same interval. Every recipe at or above the knee now warms the machine this way. The finding is also a question for users: a program that decodes after a short pause may meet the slow regime, and whether a decoder should respond to it is open until more machines are measured.

## 7\. Designing for a purpose

A question about method came next: whether the decoder could be studied the way systems biology studies a regulatory network, with Uri Alon's network motifs as the obvious model. The answer that held up was not his catalogue of recurring motifs but the line of work that asks which structures can perform a given function at all ([the lead](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/leads/structure-and-function.md>); [the outside answer](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/external/chatgpt-alon-motifs.md>)). [Ma and colleagues](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2009.06.013>) enumerated every three-node network topology to find the few able to achieve biochemical adaptation, and [Araujo and Liotta](<https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-04151-6>) later gave the solution space a basis, showing that every network capable of robust perfect adaptation is built from two kinds of module. [Khammash](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cels.2021.05.020>) states the programme in five steps: specify the function, derive necessary conditions, characterise the solution space, check realisability, and establish optimality ([an explanation](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/external/chatgpt-khammash.md>)). Ours maps onto it closely:

| Khammash's step | Ours, already | What is missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The function | the referee |  |
| Necessary conditions | G1, and records 118 and 119 | a necessity statement in positive form |
| The solution space |  | an enumeration, and then a basis |
| Realisability | the four-arm and profile ceilings |  |
| Optimality | the performance work |  |

The proposal that follows is to enumerate the referee's own class of decoders (small cursor machines with a fixed number of passes that invert every short string over a tiny alphabet), reduce the list by symmetry, cluster the survivors, look for the condition that separates them, and use a SAT solver to prove "no survivor" within the class ([journal §65](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>)). Two ideas from the same reading were adopted as method: the same function can be realised by different structures, which is why the referee counts structure; and a representation change is a search for the hidden coordinate in which the work cancels.

## 8\. The dynamical layer

The decoder is "digital on analogue": discrete events, a block admitted or a buffer released, on top of continuous quantities such as occupancy and time. The language for that is the theory of hybrid dynamical systems ([the outside answer](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/external/chatgpt-dynamics.md>); [journal §67](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>)), and Goebel, Sanfelice and Teel's book on it was read in full, with notes in our own words. Its plainest product is a list of five levers. Any reorganisation of the decoder changes one of five things ([the levers](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/reading/hybrid-dynamical-systems/relevance/levers.md>)):

1. how fast work runs (the flow map);
2. where work may proceed (the flow set);
3. when events may happen (the jump set);
4. what events do (the jump map);
5. what is represented (the state space).

Of a bound that was not a certificate

One reading of the book went too far, and an independent review caught it before it guided any experiment ([the review](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/external/chatgpt-hybrid-systems-review.md>); [journal §70](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md>)). The bottleneck maximum, the largest of the scanner's, the workers' and the reader's demands, is only a lower bound on the time a decode takes, because it ignores dependencies, starvation and blocking. One block that takes a 1 s scan, a 1 s decode and a 1 s delivery in series has a maximum of 1 s and takes 3. The notes now keep three quantities apart: lower bounds, used to prune; predictors, used to rank, and validated on held-out runs rather than by replaying a run with its own durations; and proved upper bounds, for which backward induction over a finite, acyclic state graph is the concrete route.

## 9\. How the work is done

The method is written down so that it can be reused ([the protocol](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/process/protocol.md>)). It starts from a measured surprise rather than a technique, poses the problem and its referee before any solution, and prefers a model that predicts from little data and is checked on data it did not see. Sources are read in full and saved. Several readers and reviewers answer independently before comparing, their answers are recorded verbatim, and decisions are kept in separate files with their reasons. Wrong claims are corrected in place and in public, naming who found the error, as the worker-count error and the lower bound above were. Every run registers its decision rule before its data, changes are priced jointly because a relieved bottleneck moves, and when it moves, the ideas it may have masked are tested again.

## 10\. What runs next

Five runs are planned, in order ([the programme of experiments](<https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/61f960932ea45b709b3a9aeca35599bc6bad21b3/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/briefs/experiment-programme.md>)): the stage 1 baseline, now done; the scanner's copy fix, as a paired comparison at 8, 9 and 10 workers; stage 2, which slows each activity in turn to say which controls the wall at which count; the recalibration of the worker count, including 7 against 8 and the kernel chosen below 8; and the joint frontier of scanner and worker improvements together. Both parts of stage 2 are in ([§6](<#6-stage-2-which-activity-controls-the-wall>)), and the tests they priced, the copy fix and the trim to 8 workers, are being asked again with the clock warmed first. Each cost it finds controlling will be classed as engineering, a matter of how the work is done, or as a candidate for mathematics, a matter of what work is done.

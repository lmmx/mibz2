Between the routes: Hilbert's lost problem and three ways to undo the BWT¶

Hilbert's lost 24th problem, revived in an essay against AI mathematics, names what links our three decoders and what none of them can remove.

24 November 2026 · 13 min read · Meta

In October the essayist doomslide published "Against Mathematics", a long polemic about what machines are doing to the subject. Its charge is that mathematics is coming apart into results and reasons, and that machines now produce the first without the second. Underneath the polemic the essay draws three distinctions, and the third of them, a problem Hilbert wrote down and never published, turns out to be a precise question about mibz2's decoders.

This post sets out the essay's distinctions (§1), our three ways of undoing bzip2's block sort (§2), two proposals that readings of the essay produced and what each amounts to (§3), the transformation between our decoders that Hilbert's problem asks for (§4), what a faster fourth decoder would have to be (§5), and where the question stands (§6). Two models' readings of the essay, and an audit of the records that answered them, are kept verbatim in our source record; the post draws on all of them.

1. The essay: result, proof and method¶

The essay tells the history of mathematics as a creature with three heads, each paid at a different rate:

  • the proud head discovers, and is paid in credit, so it must be quick;
  • the blind head checks, slowly and unpaid;
  • the slow head understands, and is paid only once a result is old enough to teach.

Proof assistants have already replaced the blind head: a machine checks every line without understanding it. The essay reads this year's AI results as the proud head's turn: a batch of 722 machine-written manuscripts released on 6 October, many with machine-checked proofs and nobody to explain them. Those reports are the essay's.

Three older ideas carry its argument, and they are what this post uses:

  • Hearers and Learners. After Pythagoras died, his followers split. The Hearers kept his sayings and dropped the reasons; the Learners kept both. Knowing that and knowing why became two traditions.
  • Archimedes' Method. Archimedes sent the librarian at Alexandria not only his results and their proofs, but the mechanical method that found them, so that others might "discover other theorems in addition, which have not yet occurred to me". A result, its proof and the method that generates more results are three different things.
  • Hilbert's lost 24th problem. Hilbert planned a 24th problem for his 1900 list and left it in a notebook: a theory of the simplicity of proofs. Its key sentence is an instruction: "Given two routes, it is not right to take either of these two or to look for a third; it is necessary to investigate the area lying between the two routes."

2. Three routes back from the BWT¶

mibz2 decodes bzip2, and the expensive step is undoing the Burrows–Wheeler transform (BWT). The transform sorts every rotation of a block of text and keeps the last letter of each, the column L. Undoing it means following a map called LF, which takes a row of the sorted table to the row one letter earlier in the text. With C[c] the number of letters smaller than c,

\[LF(x) = C[L[x]] + \#\{\,t < x : L[t] = L[x]\,\}.\]

We have three exact routes along LF, at very different costs:

  • the walk applies LF one row at a time;
  • Cells, our Rauzy–Veech kernel, moves whole stretches of rows at once, wherever neighbouring rows share their letter;
  • Open (Open Returns) does the same for thick stretches and walks the thin ones.

No route can shortcut a width-1 row, a run of one letter whose neighbours differ, so no stretch carries it. On our Wikidata benchmark most of these rows come from random statement IDs. Our mathematician agent has proved a lower bound, G1: in the family of methods these routes belong to, every width-1 row costs at least one step, which we call an event.

3. Two proposals, and what they amount to¶

Two models read the essay and, asked for a breakthrough, each proposed one for our decoder.

Moving scattered rows together¶

The walk follows one random ID letter by letter before it reaches the next. ChatGPT proposed moving all the IDs at once: take the sorted set of rows where the IDs begin, apply LF to all of them, and group the results by the letter each row emits. Within one letter LF keeps order, and smaller letters land below larger ones, so the result is sorted again with no comparison sort.

A small case, with L = annb$na:

row 0 1 2 3 4 5 6
letter L a n n b $ n a
LF(row) 1 4 5 3 0 6 2

The scattered rows 0, 1, 3, 5 and 6 go to 1, 4, 3, 6 and 2. Grouped by letter, keeping order within each group, they are the a's (1, 2), the b (3) and the n's (4, 6): the sorted frontier 1, 2, 3, 4, 6. (This column's LF splits into three cycles, so it illustrates the ordering only, not a whole decode.)

What the frontier amounts to:

  • A known step. It is the step of batched backward search.
  • The same number of events, built record by record. Each record step composes one row with the next, matching the walk's steps one for one: G fields of d letters cost G·d events either way, as G1 requires. A packed implementation of the partition, moving many rows per machine word, is a different object and would need pricing in its own right.
  • Cheap starting rows, unpriced assembly. The rows where IDs begin lie in one contiguous range and take one scan to find. Placing each ID's letters in the output is a further job.
  • Gap counts. Within one letter c, the gap between consecutive destinations is the number of c's between the two source rows: with \(b_c = LF(x_0)\) and \(g_i\) the count of c in \([x_i, x_{i+1})\), \(LF(x_i) = b_c + \sum_{j<i} g_j\). One step of the frontier is a matter of counting letters in gaps. Many steps, carrying each ID's identity to its place in the output, is the hard part.

The count of events against their price¶

The second reading, by Claude, separated two things G1 runs together. G1 floors the number of width-1 events. Nothing floors the price of each one. A decoder that took the same events far more cheaply would sit outside the bound.

What our records say about the price:

  • Lanes, in a C harness. Running up to eight independent walks side by side over Open's cycle bought 5–14% while each step's append was branchy and variable-length. Once each append was a single 16-byte store, eight lanes cut the emit to 0.55–0.78 of its time, though either change alone barely moved it (toy-transfer). Hiding the wait for memory pays once the work per step is small.
  • Lanes in the decoder itself. mibz2 already has that store (Sixteen at a stroke); there, eight lanes were 13% faster on one thread and 5% slower once eight workers shared the processor (Fast alone, slow in a crowd).
  • Halving the dependent steps. A squared map that halved the number of dependent steps emitted 1.22 times as slowly per step on ten workers, and the whole decode took about 1.3 times as long. It also changed the footprint and the preprocessing, and recorded no miss counts.

So neither the event count nor the count of memory waits sets the time on its own. The work in each step and the waits between steps interact, and only the whole decoder's time decides. The price per event is an open question in its own right.

4. Between the routes¶

Hilbert's instruction, put to mibz2, asks: what transformation turns the walk into Cells, and Cells into Open? What does it keep, what does it remove, and what can it never remove? Our record answers it for these three, with proofs.

The answer is contraction along compose. Think of a decoder as tiling the block's cycle with steps. Two consecutive steps can be replaced by their composite, and wherever a whole stretch of rows stays inside runs of one letter, the composite covers the stretch in one step:

What the transformation keeps, removes and cannot touch:

Contraction along compose
It preserves the text the cycle spells; the order of the cycle; that every row is covered once
It removes one LF evaluation for every interior row of a stretch: w rows paid by one translation
Its invariant the set of width-1 rows, each a single step under every contraction
On scattered rows it carries order (the frontier) but removes nothing: one count per row

This is the shape Hilbert asked for: not a third route, but the map between the two we had, with its invariant. It also says where a fourth decoder would have to come from. An event disappears only where LF applied to a set of rows has a description much shorter than the set. Stretches of one letter are one such family. The open question is whether a scattered set, such as the rows of the random IDs, has another.

One limit is sharp. The IDs' letters are random, about 4 bits a row, so any description of where those rows go needs at least 4 bits per row. That bounds the size of the description, not the number of events needed to apply it. A description applied in few, wide steps isn't ruled out.

5. What a faster fourth route would have to be¶

Not a short proof. Lower bounds on proof size are classically escaped by abbreviation: name a composite once and reuse it, as extension variables shorten propositional proofs. G1 already charges shared work only once, and survives it. Nor is a short statement enough. The formula in §2 is already a one-line rule for every row of every block, and applying it row by row is the walk. Short to state, short to prove and cheap to execute are three different properties.

An interface with its costs exposed. A candidate would have to take the form

\[\mathrm{Build}(L) \to z, \qquad \mathrm{Advance}(z, h) \to z', \qquad \mathrm{Emit}(z') \to T,\]

with a fixed language for the description z, its size, and the cost of building, composing and reading it, every access to L counted. If Emit's first act is to enumerate rows and rank each one, the candidate has moved the walk, not removed it. A useful test is to supply z for free and look at what consumes it.

Carrying identities, not only sets. Compressing the set of rows is not enough. For the set of all rows, \(LF^h\) of the set is the set itself, a constant description for every h, and nothing is decoded. What a decoder needs is the labelled map \(x \mapsto LF^h(x)\), carrying each start's identity to its place in the output.

Beyond the swap argument. Our lower bounds for rules that read only aggregate counts rest on swaps: two letters exchanged so that a column stays valid, every count is unchanged, and the output differs. A cheap order-sensitive summary defeats every such swap. With letters coded as numbers, the first moment

\[A(L) = \sum_i i \cdot \mathrm{code}(L[i])\]

changes by \((i-j)(\mathrm{code}(L[j]) - \mathrm{code}(L[i])) \ne 0\) under any swap of unequal letters at \(i\) and \(j\), and a run of code \(a\) over \([s, e)\) contributes \(a\,(s+e-1)(e-s)/2\), so A takes one pass over the runs. Yet it decodes nothing: bca and cab have the same letters and the same moment, A = 2, while their LF maps are [1, 2, 0] and [2, 0, 1]. Separating swaps is not decoding. A stronger lower bound has to find two valid columns that every permitted observation, taken together, fails to tell apart.

Where multi-row rules need their own induction. Extending G1 to rules that move several rows on a shared context needs a separate argument for the rows inside such a rule. In the valid column Paaabxbccddeeff$MZ, whose LF is one cycle of 18 rows, rows 1 and 3 both spell abcdef before diverging. One rule keyed on that context carries both. Their next rows, 4 and 6, are width-1 runs of b whose own six-letter contexts, bcdef$ and bcdefM, are unique. A rule keyed on a shared context at its start can carry width-1 rows inside it, so the charging argument has to cover those interior rows directly.

6. Where the question stands¶

The frozen target asks whether some exact decoder can take substantially fewer events than Open on real blocks. Open pays at least one event per retained step, not only per width-1 row, so a yes would have to carry all of the retained words, not just the random IDs.

  • The count. G1 holds for routes built by contracting stretches, with sharing. What it doesn't cover is a rule applied to a whole scattered set, with a short description that is also cheap to apply: the Build, Advance and Emit of §5.
  • The price. A separate question. The records show the price per event moving with the work in each step and with how steps overlap, not with either count alone.
  • The next test. Supply Open's existing word codes in output order, for free, and time the unchanged consumer against Open. If even that leaves too little headroom, this representation is closed. If it leaves room, the room is the budget for building the order natively.
  • Exactness. mibz2 must be exact on every block, so checking a guessed decoding against bzip2's 32-bit block checksum, which would accept a wrong guess about once in four billion, is not a route.

The essay's distinction between a result and its method comes out here as a concrete object. The three routes are results. Contraction along compose is a method: it explains why each route costs what it does, and names the one thing, the width-1 rows, that any new method must find another way to carry.

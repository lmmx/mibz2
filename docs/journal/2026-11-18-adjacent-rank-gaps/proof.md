# Local minima and adjacent rank gaps

Let an ordered permutation on $N$ integer ranks be given as $k$ **atoms**. On an atom $[x,x+w)$ it sends $x+i$ to $y+i$. Its label is a common word in forward output order. An LF step reads that word backwards. If an orbit successively uses atoms with forward labels $u,v$, its combined forward label is $vu$. The initial labels are the bytes of the BWT last column. The following construction also applies to ordered permutations that come from other sources.

## Choosing a section from the order

Conjugate LF by $x\mapsto(x-p)\bmod n$, where $p$ is the bzip2 primary. Cut a domain and an image interval wherever they cross the new origin. If the original column has $r$ maximal runs, the translated presentation has $R\le r+2$ atoms. All subsequent orders are restrictions of this one order. Call coordinates in it **original ranks**.

On each cycle of length greater than one, keep the ranks strictly smaller than both their neighbours, in either direction around the cycle. Every such cycle has a minimum, and two consecutive ranks on it cannot both be kept. In particular rank zero survives until its cycle has length one. Map each kept rank to the next kept rank in LF order and attach the forward label of the whole excursion. This is a permutation on the section. A fixed rank outside the marked orbit can be discarded. When the mark is fixed, its label is the reversal of one complete backward traversal from the primary. Repeating this forward word and taking the first $n$ bytes gives mibz2's marked output, including when the period fails to divide $n$ for an arbitrary last column. Thus the remaining row count at least halves on every nonterminal round.

For an ordinary primitive text in unrotated suffix order, these local minima are the cyclic counterpart of leftmost S-type suffixes in induced suffix sorting. The comparison that selects them is still meaningful after we rotate rank order to retain the mark or pass to an arbitrary ordered permutation. Here the incoming order is known from the beginning. [Nong, Zhang and Chan](https://doi.org/10.1109/TC.2010.188)

## Computing one entire round from intervals

An atom translates by $\delta=y-x$. For $\delta>0$, a trajectory entering this atom from another atom must enter through its first $e=\min(w,\delta)$ rows. Every other row of the atom already has a predecessor inside it. From an entrance rank $t$ the number of consecutive applications is

$$h(t)=\left\lceil\frac{x+w-t}{\delta}\right\rceil.$$

The exit rank is $t+h(t)\delta$. On the entrance interval $h$ has at most two values, each on a contiguous subinterval. For $\delta<0$, write $d=-\delta$. The entrances are the last $\min(w,d)$ rows and $h(t)=1+\lfloor(t-x)/d\rfloor$. A zero translation consists of fixed points. Each nonzero atom therefore gives at most two interval translations carrying a power of its old label. This division handles a trajectory passing through the same atom a million times at the same price as two consecutive visits in the word grammar.

Cut these exit intervals where they cross target-atom boundaries. The exit intervals over all atoms are disjoint. Every entrance row in the target has exactly one arriving trajectory. Consequently there are at most $3k$ resulting intervals, called **ports** in the program. Each has a source atom, a target atom, an entrance width and a word. A downwards port arriving at an upwards atom lands precisely on a local minimum. Number all these arrivals in increasing target rank to assign the compact ranks of the new section.

Start each such arrival with its interval of compact origins and an empty word. Process all upwards atoms in increasing rank, then all downwards atoms in decreasing rank. At an atom, concatenate its incoming lists in destination order. Split the list at its outgoing port widths, in source order. Prepend each port's word to that part. Send it to its destination or finish it at the next local minimum. An upwards trajectory can enter only a higher atom until it turns downwards. A downwards trajectory can enter only a lower atom until it terminates. This proves the topological order and shows that every list is available when it is read. Inside a port all ranks retain their order. A finished interval therefore gives both the translation between compact ranks and the word for every represented return.

The implementation treats each list as an AVL rope of origin intervals. A subtree has its total width and a pending word applying to every leaf. Splitting inside a leaf creates one extra origin interval. Concatenating whole lists and prepending a word create no new origin intervals. The total number of finished leaves is at most the number of initial origin intervals plus the number of port cuts, which is $O(k)$. Join and split by weight take $O(\log(2k))$ time each. A power is one grammar rule, with its child and expanded length. Concatenations created while pushing pending words are charged to the AVL operations. Sorting the ports by destination and the finished intervals by origin fits the same time bound. This proves $O(k\log(2k))$ time and grammar operations for the round with $O(k)$ interval scratch. Wide intervals and tall excursions enter through arithmetic on their widths and heights.

Locally consistent parsing has also been used to construct run-length grammars from shared substrings. Here consistency comes from a shared ordering of ranks across adjacent LF trajectories. It yields a bound on the cost to construct this particular parse from run records. [Navarro, Ochoa and Prezza](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09517)

## A certificate that can merge different word expressions

For each current atom store the original rank of its first row. The atom's rows are consecutive in original rank. Also store a bit at its first row that says whether its excursion ever lands on the **start of an initial atom before taking that elementary LF step**. Interior rows have this bit clear. Initially all atom starts have it set.

To propagate the bit, take the OR over the old excursions being concatenated. At most the first row of an outgoing port can visit its source atom's first row. In an upward passage this can happen only at its first step, in a downward passage only at its last. The division above already supplies the test. Set the bit on the leftmost origin leaf of that port when required. A split inside an origin interval leaves the new right leaf's bit clear. These operations propagate the exact bit at the cost already charged to the round.

Consider neighbouring finished intervals with adjacent original source ranks, adjacent compact images and equal return lengths in original LF steps. If the right interval's bit is clear, its first row can be shifted left by one at *every* elementary step. Both rows then use the same initial atom at each step and their elementary images remain adjacent. Hence both words are equal, even when their grammar expressions differ. Merge the intervals and retain the left expression and bit. The right operand's interior already has the same guarantee. This also proves the original-rank and interior-bit invariants for the next round. The equality test uses coordinates, length and one bit. It requires no hashing or spelling comparison.

## Why the original run count bounds every later round

Form a graph on the $n-1$ original **gaps**. Gap $g$ represents ranks $(g-1,g)$ for $1\le g<n$. Give $g$ an edge to $f(g)$ if $g$ is interior to an initial atom of $f$. On this edge $f(g-1)=f(g)-1$, and the two elementary labels agree. These edges are a partial bijection between the internal gaps of the domain atoms and those of their images. There are $n-R$ edges, $R-1$ vertices missing an incoming edge and $R-1$ missing an outgoing edge. Hence there are exactly $R-1$ finite path components, including isolated vertices. All other components are cycles.

Along any gap path, its two tracks have original ranks $z_t$ and $z_t-1$ at successive times. **The relative order of any two positions is identical on both tracks.** Along a cyclic component the choice of local minima and the weights between them therefore agree at every level. A surviving upper rank on such a component has an adjacent surviving lower rank with the same return length and adjacent image, and the gap stays internal throughout the excursion.

Here is the endpoint count for a finite path. At level zero, take its entire time list as a common consecutive list on the two tracks. To get a common list at the next level, take the local minima with both neighbours *inside* the old common list. They are selected on both tracks and are consecutive there after filtering. At every level there is at most one extra selected upper rank at each end outside the common list. Inductively, at the left end only that one possible extra rank and the old common list's first rank can be selected without two common neighbours. They are consecutive candidates, so at most one survives. The same argument holds at the right end. If the common list becomes empty, at most two upper ranks remain in this part of the orbit, and future filtering can only remove them. The lower track has the same endpoint bound. The argument also covers a cycle reduced to one fixed rank, which contributes no further strict minimum.

Between consecutive common ranks the two tracks take exactly the same number of original LF steps, stay adjacent, and arrive at adjacent surviving ranks. At the upper starting rank the certificate is clear. If a finished-interval boundary occurs there, the merge test removes it. At most **three** upper ranks on a finite gap path can therefore begin an atom at any level, one extra at each end and the last common rank whose next return might leave the path. Every positive original rank belongs as an upper rank to exactly one gap component. Original rank zero contributes at most one additional start. This proves

$$k_j\le 1+3(R-1)=3R-2$$

for every level $j$. The bound counts the intervals the executable retains after its certificate merges. It allows the count to grow from one level to the next. For example, take source widths $(1,6,1,4)$ with four distinct labels and image order $(3,0,2,1)$ using zero-based atom names. The first contraction has five certified intervals.

Write $m_j$ for the remaining number of rows. Since $k_j\le m_j\le n/2^j$, summing $\min(3R-2,n/2^j)$ gives $O(R(1+\log(n/R)))$ atom visits over the full construction. In a word-RAM with ranks and lengths in a machine word, the constructive bound from the maximal run list to the marked run-length straight-line grammar is

$$O\!\left(R(1+\log(n/R))\log(2R)\right).$$

The grammar has at most that many rules and the live interval data occupy $O(R)$ words. During writing, the first expansion of a reachable rule records its output position. Later occurrences copy from there. Powers copy a completed prefix by doubling. Including the grammar's bookkeeping, materializing the first $n$ bytes costs the construction bound plus $O(n)$. Halving counts sequential contraction rounds. It gives no parallel-time guarantee for the within-round routing.

## Accounting for the bzip2 input

The direct implementation reads the usual scanner's Huffman symbols. A RUNA/RUNB integer extends the current MTF-front byte by its full width, and a nonzero MTF index emits the next byte and moves it to the front. The scalar MTF list has at most 256 entries. With $s$ decoded Huffman symbols this step takes $O(s)$ time and $O(r)$ additional run storage for the fixed byte alphabet. Within each maximal run the repeated front byte is coded in binary, so a valid block also satisfies $s=O(r(1+\log(n/r)))$, counting the end-of-block symbol. The scanner still parses its headers, selectors and code tables. The production-compatible kernel instead accepts the existing records capped at 127 and charges a scan of all of them. Both executables perform RLE1 and the CRC checks. The final expanded size may exceed the BWT block length and its bytes still have to be delivered.

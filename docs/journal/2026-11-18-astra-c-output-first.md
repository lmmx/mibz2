# Astra C · Can the marked cursor supply its own byte sources?

18 November 2026. Independent investigation for MONODROMY and the Researcher.

**The marked output can supply its own byte sources.** Starting directly from a BWT byte interval, the cursor emits a phrase, learns which other rows spell those bytes and advance by the same displacement, and uses the completed output at later visits. On frozen, a comma-delimited version starts reusing bytes at offset 1,006. It emits all 899,931 marked bytes and reaches the published raw digest. On a full-period block this version eventually fills at least all but one of its comma return slots.

**The cost result is mixed.** Across the nine lazy-admitted sample blocks the section saves 37.2% of modeled 256 KiB line transfers. Demand-read fills rise 24.9% at that level and 86.9% at 32 KiB. Lower write traffic gives a concrete bandwidth opportunity at 7–10 workers. The exact counts do not establish an i9 speedup. ## Marked convention and first experiment

Let $L$ be the input last column, $p$ its zero-based primary, $T$ stable LF and $S=T^{-1}$. Starting at row $x=p$, emit the first-column byte $F[x]=L[S(x)]$, replace $x$ by $S(x)$, and continue for exactly $n$ bytes. This is the order of the pinned `bwt::walk` used by `Plain::invert`. A short marked cycle repeats, including a final partial traversal. Other cycles need no output addresses. For example, `L=100` at `p=0` has the single cycle (0,1,2) in forward order and spells `001`. Different marks in a column can spell different words.

**Proof.** Stable occurrence matching sends the $j$-th occurrence of $c$ in $L$ to the $j$-th row of the $c$-bucket in $F$. A maximal input run therefore gives one interval in that bucket on which $S(x)=x+d$ and $F[x]=c$. Counting byte totals and scattering one descriptor per coalesced run by byte and occurrence order constructs these local laws directly from the run input. A lookup at the current row gives both the next letter and the next row. Its output destination is the cursor. This already answers the existence question, at the price of a serial row lookup per byte. The question for the real blocks is which of these lookups can be eliminated, including the price of recognizing the opportunity.

The first experiment certifies several steps of the cursor at once. The intersection of the translated input intervals along a path is an interval of starting rows. On it both the emitted bytes and the translation to the row after the path agree. A previously completed occurrence in the output can then supply the bytes, and the current cursor supplies their destination. The cost test counts construction of certificates from the input, misses, successful queries, source reads, writes and the serial dependence through the cursor. The diagnostic screen uses dense inversion to count opportunities and check bytes.

## First frozen screen · what the continuation row costs

**Theorem.** Fix a length $k$ and a byte word $w$. The starting rows spelling $w$ form an interval. On that interval $S^k$ is strictly increasing and $S^k(x)-x$ is nondecreasing. Two rows spelling $w$ can share a single additive exit exactly when they lie in the same maximal interval with the same sequence of input pieces. To see this, $S$ is the select function on each first-column byte bucket. It is increasing there, and the distance between two rows cannot shrink. By induction, the rows spelling $c v$ are the intersection of the $c$-bucket and the monotone preimage of the interval for $v$. For two rows spelling the same word, propagate their distance through its letters. Equal initial and final distances force equality at every intermediate step. A select preserves the distance exactly when the entire intervening range of occurrences lies in a single consecutive run of $L$. Conversely a sequence of such translations preserves every distance. This proves the claim even for multiple LF cycles. It applies separately to each word of a prefix-free stopping rule.

The distinction is observable on `L=010`. Rows 0 and 1 both emit 0, yet their exit rows are 0 and 2. An offset learned at the first row would send the second to 1 and emit a spurious second zero. The dense marked outputs at primaries 0 and 1 are respectively `000` and `010`. In the latter case the marked period is 2 in a block of 3 rows.

**Exact diagnostic counts.** Take consecutive chunks of length $k$, or stop at the next quote or comma, including the delimiter, with a 64-byte cap. A final partial chunk is included. Give the experiment free recognition of every previously seen (word, displacement) pair. On a miss walk the whole chunk, on a hit copy an earlier chunk. The dense stable-sort orbit is used *only* to count the distinct keys and to certify the frozen hashes. For fixed lengths this counts exact keys for the observed length, including the tail. An actual decoder can also truncate a cached longer chunk at the end. Delimiter-aligned starts give more reuse than fixed output alignment here.

## Joint construction from completed output

At a miss, let $s$ be the starting row, $x$ the current row and $[a,b)$ its input piece. Intersect the running certificate with $[a+s-x,b+s-x)$, write the piece's byte at the cursor and advance by its displacement. At a delimiter or the cap, the intersection is the full interval for the itinerary. Store its translation $x_{\rm end}-s$, length and starting output position. On a hit the saved spelling lies entirely in the completed prefix, so the next bytes can be copied immediately. This proves readiness by induction on the cursor. A final hit can be shorter than the certificate because there is no subsequent query. A final incomplete miss needs no publication. The proof requires neither a known period nor a test for returning to the primary.

Two complete certificates for the same prefix-free stopping rule have equal or disjoint domains. The first differing base piece splits them. Within a common piece sequence the emitted bytes give the same stopping decision. This lets a simple directory clip public certificates to 64-row pages, with a head per page and one 16-byte linked record per nonempty intersection. A page has at most 64 records. The example `L=10000`, primary 0, makes the first factor `00` at row 0. Its certificate also contains row 2. The next two output bytes can come from offset 0 and row 2 advances straight to row 4. By contrast the full-cycle column `10010` spells `00` at rows 0 and 1 but their exits are 2 and 4. ## A section identifiable before decoding

There is a different choice of coordinates for a delimiter. The rows *before* emitting a comma form one known interval $A=[C[\text{comma}],C[\text{comma}]+f)$ in the first column. Consider the first return from $A$ to $A$. On a miss the cursor emits the initial comma and its following gap, accumulating the usual certificate. Intersect it once more with $A$ translated back by the terminal displacement. Every row in the result is now known to return for the **first** time at this same step. Completed return cylinders are disjoint, so explicitly publishing an entry in *every* covered row costs at most $f$ publications in the entire run. A direct array of $f$ entries is addressable as soon as the input histogram is available. The proof removes linked recognition without requiring the return map before output starts. It may leave a nearly complete map on that section when the output ends.

**Early frozen screen.** The histogram gives $A=[141903,173020)$ and $f=31,117$. An eight-byte slot for every possible query takes 248,936 bytes. The marked stream begins with six non-comma bytes and ends with a 16-byte final suffix starting at a comma. The 31,116 complete comma-to-comma gaps contain 2,893 distinct spellings and 5,251 joint word/exit classes. Walking one representative of each costs 206,616 byte steps. The longest complete gap is 284 bytes. This construction bound justified checking the section independently on the sparse input. ## What must eventually be learned

**First-return proof.** Starting in $A$, take at least one step. Every intermediate base piece is entirely inside or outside $A$, since $A$ is a first-column byte bucket. The intersected path certificate therefore excludes all earlier returns. If the final displacement is $d$, intersect once more with $[A_{\rm lo}-d,A_{\rm hi}-d)$. This last condition makes every terminal row a member of $A$. The resulting interval is maximal for the same spelling and affine exit. One can also apply the word/exit theorem to the spelling with a final lookahead comma. The comma is tested at the exit and emitted at the next visit.

The final clip is necessary. In `L=100`, take zero as the anchor. The section is $[0,2)$. From row 0, one zero leads to row 1 and returns. The initial piece would admit both 0 and 1, but moving from 1 by the same displacement leads to row 2, outside the section. Clipping leaves $[0,1)$. Across the small tests 1,452 anchor-row paths strictly require the final clip. The checker enumerates the actual word/exit classes of *all* anchor rows, checks the maximal intervals, and compares every emitted byte at every primary. Among its 28,418 marked checks are 4,516 where the anchor exists in the input and the marked cycle never visits it, and 6,872 with a partial final hit. [Section transducer](2026-11-18-astra-c-output-first/section.py) · [Independent interval check](2026-11-18-astra-c-output-first/section_small.py) · [Small receipts](2026-11-18-astra-c-output-first/section-small.json)

**Full-period accounting.** If the marked orbit visits every row and $f>0$, it visits all $f$ anchor rows exactly once in $n$ emissions. Every lookup that hits already has a published entry. Every miss publishes at least its own entry except possibly the last lookup, whose bytes finish the output. Hence between $f-1$ and $f$ entries are filled by the end. This is a lower bound on the *eventual* table for this direct-address representation. It places no lower bound on when an entry is learned. All ten blocks attained $f-1$. On frozen the first copy starts at output offset 1,006 and the final certificate is discovered at 899,731. The result is an online construction of almost the entire comma return map. The stored byte source is a representative occurrence. Its address need not be the text coordinate of the row using it. The earlier page-directory construction, in comparison, publishes intervals covering only 35,706 of 899,931 original rows.

**A family without reuse.** Form the cyclic text by concatenating one `a` followed by $j$ copies of `b`, for $j=1,\ldots,m$. The longest gap occurs once, so the text is primitive and its cyclic BWT has one LF orbit. Mark the first `a`. With anchor `a`, all gap spellings have distinct lengths. This cursor walks all $n$ bytes and copies none despite the simple gap descriptions. Five members from $m=1$ through $32$ are in the executable checks. If an anchor is absent from the marked cycle, the initial prefix walk likewise emits all $n$ bytes.

**Frozen execution.** The section cursor makes 31,117 probes, 25,865 successful output copies and 206,638 single steps. The copied 693,293 bytes and the directly emitted bytes sum to the complete 899,931-byte inverse. The 5,251 published certificates include 3,949 singleton intervals. An eight-byte entry packs 20-bit source and positive length fields and a biased 21-bit displacement, sufficient throughout bzip2's 900,000-row range. Zero means empty. The array is initialized once and each occupied slot is written once. The trailing partial miss publishes nothing. The standard RLE1 expansion of the resulting bytes produces 900,000 bytes matching the published libbz2 digest. **Interpretation.** Across the nine lazy-admitted blocks there are 996,974 fewer 256 KiB transfers and 217,475 more demand-read fills at that capacity. At 32 KiB the saving is 934,246 transfers with 1,507,967 extra demand-read fills. In the simple score “one unit per transfer plus an extra charge per demand-read fill”, the respective break-even extra charges are 4.58 and 0.62 transfer units. Those exchange rates describe sensitivities, not i9 latency prices. Every paired block uses fewer transfers at both capacities, but the smallest 32 KiB saving against lazy is 0.17%. Repeated misses along one marked cursor can matter even when fewer dirty lines contend for bandwidth.

A stricter existence check dispenses even with the global one-step select index. A 256-entry histogram supplies the next byte and its ordinal. Scan the capped input to select that occurrence, then repeat at the returned row. The final byte needs no select. On a full orbit every row of $L$ is selected once except the primary. Consequently, if capped record $i$ has width $w_i$ and the primary is in record $h$, this version reads exactly $\sum_i(i+1)w_i-(h+1)$ caps after the histogram. Frozen gives **32,808,502,739 cap reads**, or 65,617,005,478 logical bytes. That is 1,025,699,903 sequential line spans over repeated scans. The input fits a private cache at some levels, so the byte total is not external traffic. The fast structural models above do pay to compile a *whole-column, one-step* run select index. This scan establishes existence under the stricter reading, and falsifies its practical cost. **Disposition.** Correct marked order, directly emitted bytes and usable copy sources can be discovered together from compressed input. The comma section establishes a substantial modeled transfer saving on these real blocks, while the increased read fills and serial dependency chain leave its decode speed open. This is a structural result for independent verification. The study stops at the run-input transducers and address traces. No native timing or production change is part of the result.

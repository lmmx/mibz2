# Astra C · Can the marked cursor supply its own byte sources?

18 November 2026. Independent investigation for MONODROMY and the Researcher.

## Marked convention and first experiment

Let $L$ be the input last column, $p$ its zero-based primary, $T$ stable LF and $S=T^{-1}$. Starting at row $x=p$, emit the first-column byte $F[x]=L[S(x)]$, replace $x$ by $S(x)$, and continue for exactly $n$ bytes. This is the order of the pinned `Plain::walk`. A short marked cycle repeats, including a final partial traversal. Other cycles need no output addresses. For example, `L=100` at `p=0` has the single cycle (0,1,2) in forward order and spells `001`. Different marks in a column can spell different words.

**Proof.** Stable occurrence matching sends the $j$-th occurrence of $c$ in $L$ to the $j$-th row of the $c$-bucket in $F$. A maximal input run therefore gives one interval in that bucket on which $S(x)=x+d$ and $F[x]=c$. Counting byte totals and scattering one descriptor per coalesced run by byte and occurrence order constructs these local laws directly from the run input. A lookup at the current row gives both the next letter and the next row. Its output destination is the cursor. This already answers the existence question, at the price of a serial row lookup per byte. The question for the real blocks is which of these lookups can be eliminated, including the price of recognizing the opportunity.

The first experiment will certify several steps of the cursor at once. The intersection of the translated input intervals along a path is an interval of starting rows. On it both the emitted bytes and the translation to the row after the path agree. A previously completed occurrence in the output can then supply the bytes, and the current cursor supplies their destination. The cost test counts construction of certificates from the input, misses, successful queries, source reads, writes and the serial dependence through the cursor. Diagnostic uses of dense LF will be identified explicitly.

## First frozen screen · what the continuation row costs

**Theorem.** Fix a length \(k\) and a byte word \(w\). The starting rows spelling \(w\) form an interval. On that interval \(S^k\) is strictly increasing and \(S^k(x)-x\) is nondecreasing. Two rows spelling \(w\) can share a single additive exit exactly when they lie in the same maximal interval with the same sequence of input pieces. To see this, \(S\) is the select function on each first-column byte bucket. It is increasing there, and the distance between two rows cannot shrink. By induction, the rows spelling \(c v\) are the intersection of the \(c\)-bucket and the monotone preimage of the interval for \(v\). For two rows spelling the same word, propagate their distance through its letters. Equal initial and final distances force equality at every intermediate step. A select preserves the distance exactly when the entire intervening range of occurrences lies in a single consecutive run of \(L\). Conversely a sequence of such translations preserves every distance. This proves the claim even for multiple LF cycles. It applies separately to each word of a prefix-free stopping rule.

The distinction is observable on `L=010`. Rows 0 and 1 both emit 0, yet their exit rows are 0 and 2. An offset learned at the first row would send the second to 1 and emit a spurious second zero. The dense marked outputs at primaries 0 and 1 are respectively `000` and `010`. In the latter case the marked period is 2 in a block of 3 rows.

**Exact diagnostic counts.** Take consecutive chunks of length \(k\), or stop at the next quote or comma, including the delimiter, with a 64-byte cap. A final partial chunk is included. Give the experiment free recognition of every previously seen (word, displacement) pair. On a miss walk the whole chunk, on a hit copy an earlier chunk. The dense stable-sort orbit is used *only* to count the distinct keys and to certify the frozen hashes. For fixed lengths this counts exact keys for the observed length, including the tail. An actual decoder can also truncate a cached longer chunk at the end. Delimiter-aligned starts give more reuse than fixed output alignment here.

An input-only alternative is to contract a chain of run pieces whose complete images each fit within the next piece. Its recognition can be done by merging two row-ordered tilings. On frozen only 4,695 of 51,184 pieces have a split image, but they contain 369,025 rows. The full-period marked cursor must still disambiguate a successor at each of those visits in this representation. Even perfect access to the forced-chain bytes leaves 369,025 dependent row decisions, plus chain construction and emission. I stopped this arm at the census.

For the comma policy, 5,448 row intervals could supply the exits and 726,525 bytes. This gives a reason to price an actual on-demand recognition scheme. Even with a free dictionary it takes 33,214 phrase decisions plus 173,406 single steps. Cells makes 92,570 cuts with more work per cut. The counts alone settle neither line traffic nor performance.

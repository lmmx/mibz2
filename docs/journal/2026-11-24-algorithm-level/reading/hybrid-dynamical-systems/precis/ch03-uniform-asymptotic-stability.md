# Chapter 3: Uniform asymptotic stability, an initial treatment (pp. 43–71)

The chapter defines the book's central stability property, uniform global pre-asymptotic stability (UGpAS) of a closed set, and gives the first tools for proving it. Closed sets rather than points are the natural targets because hybrid systems carry timers, modes and counters that never settle to a point. The prefix "pre" means maximal solutions are not required to be complete, which separates questions of existence and completeness from questions of stability. The chapter gives sufficient conditions via Lyapunov functions (strict and several relaxed forms), results relating stability of a system to stability of its parts, and equivalent characterisations by a class-$\mathcal{KL}$ bound and by exponential decay in suitable coordinates. None of the results here needs the well-posedness assumptions of Chapter 6; the more powerful results of Chapter 7 do.

## 3.1 Uniform global pre-asymptotic stability (p. 43)

UGpAS asks that the distance of every solution to the set be bounded in terms of the initial distance and of elapsed hybrid time $t + j$, with the bound vanishing as either the initial distance goes to zero or $t + j$ goes to infinity. Motivating sets with many points:

- **Example 3.1 (Sample-and-hold and compact attractors, p. 44).** For the sample-and-hold loop of Example 1.4, the right target is $\{0\} \times \mathbb{R}^{n_c} \times [0, T]$: plant state at zero, control and timer unconstrained (or the control confined to a compact set $U$ if the design bounds it, making the target compact).
- **Example 3.2 (Switching systems and compact attractors, p. 44).** Stability of $z = 0$ under all dwell-time signals becomes stability of $\{0\} \times Q \times [0, \tau_D]$ for (2.6); under persistent dwell-time signals, of $\{0\} \times Q \times [0,\tau_D] \times [0,T]$ for (2.7).
- **Example 3.3 (Time-varying systems, p. 44).** If time is included as a state with $\dot\tau = 1$ and $\tau^+ = \tau + 1$, then $\tau$ grows like $t + j$ and any stable set is typically unbounded.
- **Definition 3.4 (Class-$\mathcal{K}_\infty$ functions, p. 45).** Continuous, zero at zero, strictly increasing and unbounded functions on $\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}$.
- **Definition 3.5 (Distance to a closed set, p. 45).** $|x|_\mathcal{A} = \inf_{y \in \mathcal{A}} |x - y|$.
- **Definition 3.6 (UGpAS, p. 45).** For closed $\mathcal{A}$: *uniformly globally stable* if some $\alpha \in \mathcal{K}_\infty$ gives $|\phi(t,j)|_\mathcal{A} \le \alpha(|\phi(0,0)|_\mathcal{A})$ for every solution and every point of its domain; *uniformly globally pre-attractive* if for every $\varepsilon, r > 0$ there is $T$ such that every solution starting within distance $r$ is within $\varepsilon$ at all domain points with $t + j \ge T$; UGpAS if both hold. Since the attractivity clause only constrains points that exist in the domain, solutions that stop early are allowed.

The authors justify "pre": Lyapunov-type conditions do not ensure existence or completeness, even for ODEs with irregular right-hand sides, so it is cleaner to keep these questions apart.

- **Example 3.7 (Pre-attractivity in continuous time, p. 46).** A planar ODE with discontinuous right-hand side: off the $x_1$-axis it is $\dot z = -z$, while on the axis it is defined differently, in a way that admits no solution from any nonzero point of the axis (the exact formula is garbled in the extracted text). Solutions from points off the axis are $\xi e^{-t}$; the origin is UGpAS but not asymptotically stable in the usual sense, since some nearby initial points have no solution. $V = \tfrac12|z|^2$ still decreases along the field.
- **Example 3.8 (Stability that looks unstable; completing a stable system, p. 46).** On the strip $C = \mathbb{R}\times[0,M]$, flow $\dot x_1 = x_1$, $\dot x_2 = 1$, no jumps. Distance to $\mathcal{A} = \{0\}\times[0,M]$ grows exponentially, yet $\mathcal{A}$ is UGpAS because every solution lives for at most time $M$: the growth factor is at most $e^M$ and attractivity holds vacuously beyond $T = M + 1$. Adding jumps at $x_2 = M$ that reset $x_2$ to 0 and scale $x_1$ by $\lambda < e^{-M}$ makes all maximal solutions complete while keeping UGpAS; the distance then contracts by $\lambda e^M$ per cycle.
- **Example 3.9 (UGpAS with finite escape times, p. 48).** With $\dot x_1 = x_1^3$, $\dot x_2 = -x_2$ and the unbounded set $\mathcal{A} = \mathbb{R}\times\{0\}$, distance decays exponentially while $x_1$ escapes to infinity in finite time. So for unbounded sets, finite escape is compatible with UGpAS.

Pre-attractivity (all complete solutions converge) without uniformity needs one of: $\mathcal{A}$ not uniformly stable, $\mathcal{A}$ unbounded, or the data failing regularity conditions such as closed $C$ and $D$ (compare Theorem 7.12).

- **Example 3.10 (Nonuniformity because $\mathcal{A}$ is not stable, p. 48).** On $\mathbb{R}$ with $\dot x = -x(x-1)^2$ and $\mathcal{A} = \{0, 1\}$: solutions just left of 1 drift to 0, taking arbitrarily long. The interval $[0,1]$ is UGpAS, though.
- **Example 3.11 (Nonuniformity because $\mathcal{A}$ is unbounded, p. 48).** On $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{\ge1}$, $\dot x_1 = -x_1/x_2$, $\dot x_2 = 1$, $\mathcal{A} = \{0\}\times\mathbb{R}$: convergence holds but the time to shrink the distance by a given factor grows linearly with the initial $x_2$.

Discontinuous data also break uniformity: an ODE whose decay rate along rays becomes arbitrarily slow, and the map $x^+ = x^2$ on $(0,1)$ (zero elsewhere), where points just below 1 need arbitrarily many steps.

- **Example 3.12 ("Uniformly nonuniform attractivity", p. 49).** An odd map on $\mathbb{R}$ that sends a fraction $p/q$ (with $q$ prime, $p,q > 1$, coprime) to $(p-1)/q$ and everything else to 0. Every solution reaches 0 in finitely many steps, but every open set contains points needing arbitrarily many steps.
- **Example 3.13 (Nonuniformity from a jump set that is not closed, p. 49).** $C = [1,2]$, $\dot x = 1$, $D = (-\infty,1)\cup[2,\infty)$, with jumps from below 1 squaring the positive part (and from $[2,\infty)$ going to 0). Points of $D$ just below 1 need arbitrarily many jumps to approach 0.
- **Example 3.14 (Nonuniformity from a flow set that is not closed, p. 50).** $C = (-\infty,1)$ with $\dot x = x(x-1)$, $D = [1,\infty)$, $g = 0$: flow starting just below 1 takes arbitrarily long.
- **Example 3.15 (Nonuniformity from a discontinuous flow map, p. 50).** On $C = \mathbb{R}$, $\dot x = x(x-1)$ below 1 and $\dot x = -1$ from 1 upward: no solution from $x = 1$, and arbitrarily slow convergence from just below it; complete solutions still converge.

## 3.2 Lyapunov functions (p. 50)

The section first recalls the classical ODE theory: a $C^1$ positive definite $V$ that is nonincreasing along a continuous vector field gives Lyapunov stability; strict decrease gives local attractivity (using continuity to guarantee solutions exist); compact sublevel sets give global asymptotic stability. Uniformity comes from $\mathcal{K}_\infty$ sandwich bounds on $V$ and from the derivative being bounded away from zero on compact sets avoiding the origin, which must be assumed outright if $f$ is discontinuous. For hybrid systems, $V$ need only be defined where solutions can be and smooth near the flow set (smooth converse results come in Chapter 7).

- **Definition 3.16 (Lyapunov function candidate, p. 52).** $V$ is defined at least on $C \cup D \cup G(D)$ and is continuously differentiable on an open set containing the closure of $C$.
- **Definition 3.17 (Positive definite functions, p. 52).** $\rho: \mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}$ with $\rho(0) = 0$ and $\rho(s) > 0$ for $s > 0$; written $\rho \in \mathcal{PD}$.
- **Theorem 3.18 (Sufficient Lyapunov conditions, p. 52).** For closed $\mathcal{A}$ and a candidate $V$, if there are $\alpha_1, \alpha_2 \in \mathcal{K}_\infty$ and continuous $\rho \in \mathcal{PD}$ with
  (3.2a) $\alpha_1(|x|_\mathcal{A}) \le V(x) \le \alpha_2(|x|_\mathcal{A})$ on $C \cup D \cup G(D)$,
  (3.2b) $\langle \nabla V(x), f\rangle \le -\rho(|x|_\mathcal{A})$ for $x \in C$, $f \in F(x)$,
  (3.2c) $V(g) - V(x) \le -\rho(|x|_\mathcal{A})$ for $x \in D$, $g \in G(x)$,
  then $\mathcal{A}$ is UGpAS. The proof integrates the flow decrease over each flow interval and sums the jump decreases to get a bound (3.3) in which $V$ at $(t,j)$ plus the accumulated decrease is at most $V$ at the start; stability follows from the sandwich, and an explicit time $T$ (3.4) is found after which a solution must have entered a small level set, from which it cannot leave.

Remarks: such a $V$ is called a Lyapunov function and (3.2) the Lyapunov conditions. For compact $\mathcal{A}$ and continuous $V$, (3.2a) is equivalent to $V$ vanishing exactly on $\mathcal{A}$ and being radially unbounded on $C \cup D \cup G(D)$. One $\rho$ suffices for flows and jumps (take the pointwise minimum). When $F$ and $G$ are continuous, the conditions extend to the closures of $C$ and $D$, so UGpAS also holds for the system with enlarged sets.

- **Example 3.19 (Bouncing ball, p. 54).** The energy $V_1 = \tfrac12 x_2^2 + \gamma x_1$ satisfies the sandwich and strict decrease at jumps (by $\tfrac12(1-\lambda^2)x_2^2$, using $x_1 = 0$ on $D$), but is only constant during flow, so (3.2b) fails. Two modifications fix this: multiplying $V_1$ by $1 + \theta\arctan(x_2)$ with a specific $\theta$ depending on $\lambda$, or adding a small multiple of $x_2 |V_1|^{1/2}$ to $V_1$. Either satisfies Theorem 3.18, proving UGpAS of the origin.
- **Example 3.20 (Stability that looks unstable, p. 55).** For Example 3.8, $V = x_1^2 e^{-2\sigma x_2}$ with $\sigma > 1$ decreases strictly along flows; for the completed system it also works if $\sigma$ is chosen with $\lambda e^{\sigma M} < 1$, so that jumps also contract $V$.
- **Example 3.21 (Linear sampled-data systems, p. 56).** Plant $\dot z = Az + Bu$, held control, sampling period $T$, update $u^+ = Kz$. With $H = \exp(A_f T) A_g$ the sample-to-sample map, Schur stability of $H$ (a positive definite $P$ with $H^\top P H - P$ negative definite) gives $W(x_1) = x_1^\top P x_1$ decreasing per sample. The candidate $V_1(x) = W(\exp(A_f(T - \tau))x_1)$ (predicting the state at the next sample) is constant during flow and strictly decreasing at jumps; the modification $V_2 = e^{-\sigma\tau}V_1$ with small $\sigma > 0$ satisfies all of Theorem 3.18, proving UGAS of $\{x_1 = 0, \tau \in [0,T]\}$.
- **Example 3.22 (Switching systems under average dwell time, p. 57).** Using the generator of Section 2.4 with switching rate parameter $\eta$ (i.e. at most $\eta(t-s) + N_\circ$ switches in $[s,t]$), assume each mode has a quadratic-type Lyapunov function $W_q$ bounded between $e^{\underline c}|z|^2$ and $e^{\bar c}|z|^2$ (the bars are lost in the extracted text), decaying at rate $\lambda$, with $\bar c - \underline c < \lambda/\eta$. Then $V = e^{\mu\tau}W_q(z)$ with $\bar c - \underline c < \mu < \lambda/\eta$ ((3.6)) decreases during flow (rate $\mu\eta - \lambda < 0$) and at jumps (factor $e^{-\mu + \bar c - \underline c} < 1$), so the set $\{z = 0\}$ is UGpAS. The token counter absorbs the jump in $W$ when the mode changes.
- **Example 3.23 (Uniting local and global controllers, p. 58).** With Lyapunov functions $W_1$ (local, decreasing on a neighbourhood $U_1$) and $W_2$ (global) sharing $\mathcal{K}_\infty$ bounds, choose a sublevel set of $W_1$ inside $U_1$, put $D_2$ inside it, $C_2$ its complement, $C_1 = U_1$, $D_1$ the complement of $U_1$. A combined $V = \sigma_q(W_q(z))$, with $\sigma_2$ the identity and $\sigma_1$ a reshaping function chosen to make $V$ drop at both kinds of switch, satisfies Theorem 3.18, so $\{q = 1, z = 0\}$ is UGpAS.

## 3.3 Relaxed Lyapunov conditions (p. 60)

Stability only needs $V$ nonincreasing; attractivity needs $V$ to decrease over long enough hybrid time. The conditions (3.2) are restated as (3.7a)–(3.7c).

- **Proposition 3.24 (Persistent jumping, p. 60).** If (3.7a) and (3.7c) hold, $V$ is merely nonincreasing during flow ((3.8)), and jumps occur persistently in the sense that for each $r > 0$ there are $\gamma_r \in \mathcal{K}_\infty$ and $N_r \ge 0$ such that every solution starting at distance in $(0, r]$ has $j \ge \gamma_r(T) - N_r$ whenever $t + j \ge T$, then $\mathcal{A}$ is UGpAS. The proof is that of Theorem 3.18 counting only jump decreases.
- **Example 3.25 (Bouncing ball, p. 61).** $V_1$ (the energy) satisfies the hypotheses: by (2.4) total flow time from a bounded set is bounded, so hybrid time is eventually all jumps.
- **Example 3.26 (Sampled-data systems, p. 61).** $V_1$ of Example 3.21 satisfies the hypotheses since $t \le (j+1)T_s$ forces $j$ to grow linearly with $t + j$.
- **Proposition 3.27 (Persistent flowing, p. 61).** Dual of 3.24: (3.7a) and (3.7b) hold, $V$ is nonincreasing at jumps ((3.9)), and $t \ge \gamma_r(T) - N_r$ whenever $t + j \ge T$ for solutions starting at distance in $(0, r]$. Then $\mathcal{A}$ is UGpAS. The proof is omitted as nearly identical.
- **Example 3.28 (Illustrating Proposition 3.27, p. 61).** Hurwitz linear flow on $\mathbb{R}^n$, jumps $x^+ = -x$ allowed on the nonnegative orthant. $V = x^\top P x$ decreases strictly in flow and is unchanged at jumps. A jump from the orthant lands at a definite distance from it (scale invariance), so each jump is followed by at least a fixed flow time $T_f$, giving $t \ge (T-1)T_f/(T_f + 1)$; the domain condition is needed only for solutions not starting in $\mathcal{A}$.
- **Proposition 3.29 (Increase balanced by decrease, p. 62).** If (3.7a) holds, $V$ grows at most at exponential rate $\lambda_c$ in flow and by a factor $e^{\lambda_d}$ at jumps ((3.10), with $\lambda_c, \lambda_d$ real, possibly positive), and every solution's domain satisfies $\lambda_c t + \lambda_d j \le M - \gamma(t + j)$ for some $\gamma, M > 0$, then $\mathcal{A}$ is UGpAS. This lets increase in one regime be paid for by decrease in the other, given enough of the latter.
- **Proposition 3.30 (Finite number of jumps or bounded flow time, p. 62).** With (3.7a), either (1) strict decrease in flow (3.7b), jumps that increase $V$ by at most a $\mathcal{K}_\infty$ function $\lambda$ ((3.11)), and the number of jumps bounded by $\gamma(|\phi(0,0)|_\mathcal{A}) + J$ with $\gamma \in \mathcal{K}$; or (2) strict decrease at jumps (3.7c), growth in flow at most exponential ((3.12)), and total flow time bounded by some $T_r$ for solutions starting within distance $r$. Either gives UGpAS. The proof (case 1) bounds the effect of finitely many jumps on $V$ and shows that a long enough solution must contain a long flow interval on which $V$ drops sufficiently.
- **Example 3.31 (Uniting controllers revisited, p. 64).** With simply $V = W_q(z)$, each switch raises $V$ by at most $\alpha_2\circ\alpha_1^{-1}$, and the construction of $D_1, D_2$ allows at most two switches, so case (1) applies with $J = 2$.

## 3.4 Stability from containment (p. 64)

- **Proposition 3.32 (UGpAS from containment, p. 64).** If $\mathcal{H}_2$'s data are contained in $\mathcal{H}_1$'s ($C_2 \subset C_1$, $F_2 \subset F_1$ on $C_2$, $D_2 \subset D_1$, $G_2 \subset G_1$ on $D_2$), UGpAS for $\mathcal{H}_1$ implies it for $\mathcal{H}_2$, since every solution of $\mathcal{H}_2$ is a solution of $\mathcal{H}_1$.
- **Corollary 3.33 (p. 64).** UGpAS for $\mathcal{H}$ implies UGpAS for the flow-only system $(C, F, \emptyset, \emptyset)$ and for the jump-only system $(\emptyset, \emptyset, D, G)$.

The converse fails, shown three ways:

- **Example 3.34 (No converse, I, p. 64).** $\dot x = -x$ off $[1,2]$, jumps $x^+ = 3x$ on $[1,2]$. Each part alone is UGpAS (the jump-only system jumps once out of $D$), but combined, the solution from 2 jumps to 6, flows back to 2, and repeats forever, staying at least 2.
- **Example 3.35 (No converse, II, p. 65).** Flow $\dot x = 1$ on $[1,2]$; jumps $x^+ = 0.75x$ on $[0,1)\cup[2,\infty)$ (the extracted text prints a product sign here; on $\mathbb{R}$ it must be a union). Each alone is UGpAS (flow lasts at most one unit of time), but combined the solution from 2 cycles between $[1.5, 2]$ forever.
- **Example 3.36 (No converse, III, p. 65).** On $\mathbb{R}^2$ with $C = D = \mathbb{R}^2$: a lightly damped linear flow that is UGAS on its own, and a linear jump map with spectral radius $\lambda < 1$ that is UGAS on its own, both with complete solutions. For small damping and $\lambda$ near 1, flowing a quarter turn and then jumping multiplies the distance by more than 8, so some hybrid solutions grow exponentially. This is the classical instability-by-switching phenomenon.
- **Theorem 3.37 (Treating systems with "bad" jumps, p. 66).** UGpAS of $\mathcal{A}$ for $\mathcal{H}$ is equivalent to: (i) jumps are bounded relative to the set, $|g|_\mathcal{A} \le \gamma(|x|_\mathcal{A})$ for some $\gamma \in \mathcal{K}_\infty$, and (ii) UGpAS for the reduced system $\tilde{\mathcal{H}}$ whose jump map keeps only jump values landing in $C \cup D$ (and whose jump set is where that is nonempty). The reason: non-complete solutions of $\mathcal{H}$ are solutions of $\tilde{\mathcal{H}}$ with at most one terminal jump out of $C \cup D$ appended. The theorem disposes of the jump-only part of Example 3.34 at once, and applied twice handles $x^+ = 3x$ on $[1, 6]$.

## 3.5 Equivalent characterisations (p. 68)

- **Definition 3.38 (Class-$\mathcal{KL}$ functions, p. 68).** $\beta(r, s)$ nondecreasing in $r$, nonincreasing in $s$, tending to 0 as $r \to 0^+$ for each $s$ and as $s \to \infty$ for each $r$. (Continuity is not part of the definition here.)
- **Lemma 3.39 (p. 68).** Every $\beta \in \mathcal{KL}$ is dominated by some $\alpha \in \mathcal{K}_\infty$ in its first argument, uniformly in the second; the proof builds $\alpha$ piecewise linearly on dyadic intervals.
- **Theorem 3.40 (UGpAS equals a $\mathcal{KL}$ bound, p. 68).** $\mathcal{A}$ is UGpAS if and only if some $\beta \in \mathcal{KL}$ gives $|\phi(t,j)|_\mathcal{A} \le \beta(|\phi(0,0)|_\mathcal{A}, t + j)$ for all solutions and domain points. One direction builds $\beta$ as the worst case over solutions of the distance at hybrid time at least $s$ (clipped at 0 when no solution lasts that long); the other uses Lemma 3.39.
- **Lemma 3.41 (Class-$\mathcal{KL}$ and exponential decay; Massera–Sontag, p. 69).** For every $\beta \in \mathcal{KL}$ and $\lambda > 0$ there are $\alpha_1, \alpha_2 \in \mathcal{K}_\infty$ with $\alpha_1(\beta(r,s)) \le \alpha_2(r)e^{-\lambda s}$.
- **Theorem 3.42 (Equivalent characterisations of UGpAS, p. 70).** UGpAS holds if and only if, for each $\lambda > 0$, there are $\alpha_1, \alpha_2 \in \mathcal{K}_\infty$ with $\alpha_1(|\phi(t,j)|_\mathcal{A}) \le \alpha_2(|\phi(0,0)|_\mathcal{A})\,e^{-\lambda(t+j)}$ ((3.14)). Measured through a suitable nonlinear rescaling of distance, convergence is exponential in hybrid time, at any prescribed rate; in particular the $\mathcal{KL}$ bound may be taken continuous.

## 3.6 Notes (p. 71)

Definition 3.6 parallels the classical notion, which usually assumes completeness (references: Khalil; Lin, Sontag and Wang for inclusions and closed sets; Teel and Zaccarian on competing definitions for time-varying systems). Comparison functions go back to Hahn. Example 3.10 echoes Vinograd's example; Example 3.11 matches one in Khalil. Lemma 3.41 is related to Massera's lemma, based on Sontag, with a proof following Teel and Praly. The bouncing-ball Lyapunov functions come from Cai et al. and Lamperski and Ames; the average dwell-time condition is due to Hespanha and Morse. Example 3.36 is a classical switched-system instability example. Not covered here: multiple Lyapunov functions (Branicky; DeCarlo et al.) and Matrosov functions.

## Key ideas to carry away

- Stability is of a closed set, measured by distance to it; timers, modes and counters are left free inside the set.
- Elapsed hybrid time is $t + j$: convergence may come from flow, from jumps, or from both.
- "Pre" separates existence and completeness from stability: a solution that stops is not a counterexample, and a system can be UGpAS while looking unstable if its solutions cannot last.
- Uniformity can fail because the set is unstable or unbounded, or because the data are irregular (open flow or jump sets, discontinuous maps); Chapter 6's regularity conditions are what rule this out.
- A Lyapunov function need not decrease strictly in both regimes: it is enough that it never increases and that the strictly decreasing regime occurs persistently, or that growth in one regime is outweighed by decrease in the other over the domains that actually occur. Domain constraints (dwell time, bounded flow time, bounded number of jumps) are thus part of the proof.
- Stability of flows alone and jumps alone does not imply stability of the combination (Examples 3.34–3.36); restricting the data preserves stability.
- UGpAS is equivalent to a single $\mathcal{KL}$ bound in $(|x_0|_\mathcal{A}, t + j)$, and further to exponential decay at any rate in rescaled coordinates.

## Candidate uses for a discrete-event computing pipeline

*Speculative; for the team lead to evaluate.*

- Steady-state operation of the decoder is naturally a closed set, not a point: for example the set where the window occupancy, queue lengths and worker phases lie within fixed bounds, with clocks free. UGpAS of such a set is the formal statement that the pipeline settles after warm-up, uniformly over starting backlogs.
- The $\mathcal{KL}$ bound of Theorem 3.40 in $t + j$ suggests measuring convergence from the traces against both wall time and event count; event count is the right clock in burst phases (many events at little time).
- Proposition 3.24 fits a potential function such as outstanding work (issued but not consumed, weighted by remaining work) that cannot increase between events but drops at each consumed event, provided consumed events occur persistently; the exact conservation laws could supply such a function and its bounds.
- Proposition 3.29 is a template for a potential that rises at issue events and falls at completion events, stable provided the trace domains satisfy a balance inequality, which could be checked directly on the recorded event counts.
- Examples 3.34–3.36 are a warning relevant to the throughput switch near 8 workers: two regimes that are each well behaved can combine badly, so analysing each bottleneck regime separately does not prove the switching pipeline is well behaved.

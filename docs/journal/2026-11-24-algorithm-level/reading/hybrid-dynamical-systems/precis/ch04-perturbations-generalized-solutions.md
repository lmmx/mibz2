# Chapter 4: Perturbations and generalized solutions (pp. 73–95)

The chapter asks what happens to the solutions of a hybrid system $\mathcal H=(C,F,D,G)$ when the state is perturbed by an arbitrarily small error at every place it is used: in the flow and jump conditions and in the flow and jump maps. It shows that, when the data lack regularity (non-closed flow or jump sets, discontinuous maps), vanishingly small perturbations can create, destroy or radically reshape solutions, and that asymptotic stability can then be lost. It then identifies exactly what such perturbations can produce in the limit: the Hermes solutions (limits of perturbed solutions) coincide with the Krasovskii solutions (solutions of a "regularised" system built by closing the sets and convexifying and closing the maps). This equivalence is the motivation for the regularity assumptions of Chapter 6 and, through them, for the robustness theory of the rest of the book.

## 4.1 Differential and difference equations (p. 73)

The section sets up the phenomenon in the purely continuous-time and purely discrete-time cases.

- **Example 4.1** (p. 73–74). On $\mathbb R^2$ the vector field is $(1,0)$ when $z_2\ge 0$ and $(-1,0)$ when $z_2<0$. From the origin the unperturbed solution moves right at unit speed. A perturbation of size $\varepsilon$ in the second coordinate (e.g. $\varepsilon\sin t$) makes the first coordinate oscillate in a saw-tooth between $0$ and $\pi$. More generally, a periodic perturbation of period $\varepsilon$ that is $+\varepsilon$ for a fraction $\lambda\in(0,1)$ of each period and $-\varepsilon$ otherwise produces saw-tooth motion whose average slope is $2\lambda-1$; as $\varepsilon\to 0$ these converge uniformly to $z_1(t)=(2\lambda-1)t$, $z_2\equiv 0$. That limit is a solution of the differential inclusion whose right-hand side equals the single value off the switching line and the segment $[-1,1]\times\{0\}$ on it, i.e. the smallest convex set containing the nearby values.
- **Definition 4.2** (p. 74–75). For $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n$ and absolutely continuous $z$ on $[0,T]$: $z$ is a *Hermes solution* of $\dot z=f(z)$ if it is the uniform limit of absolutely continuous $z_i$ satisfying $\dot z_i=f(z_i+e_i)$ almost everywhere with measurable $e_i\to 0$ uniformly; $z$ is a *Krasovskii solution* if $\dot z(t)\in F(z(t))$ a.e., where $F(\xi)=\bigcap_{\delta>0}\overline{\mathrm{con}}\, f(\xi+\delta\mathbb B)$ (the intersection over shrinking balls of the closed convex hull of nearby values).
- **Theorem 4.3** (p. 75). If $f$ is locally bounded, an absolutely continuous arc is a Hermes solution if and only if it is a Krasovskii solution. The proof is deferred: one direction comes from Theorem 4.21, and the hybrid generalisation is Theorem 4.17.
- **Example 4.4** (p. 75–76). A discontinuous staircase map $g$ on $\mathbb R$ (shown in a figure) sends every point to zero in finitely many steps, the number of steps growing with $|\xi|$. With a constant perturbation $e(j)=\varepsilon$, the perturbed solution from $\xi'$ jumps to $\mathrm{floor}(\xi'+\varepsilon)$ and stays there. For the integer starting point shown in the figure this is $\xi'$ itself, so as $\varepsilon\to 0$ the perturbed solutions converge to the constant solution at $\xi'$, which the unperturbed map never produces. The limit solves the difference inclusion $z^+\in G(z)$, where at points of discontinuity $G$ collects all limiting values of $g$ from nearby points (here no convexification). The discrete case is otherwise subsumed by the hybrid theory of Section 4.3.

## 4.2 Systems with state perturbations (p. 76)

The section defines the perturbed hybrid system and catalogues the pathologies.

- **System (4.1)** (p. 76). $\mathcal H_e$: flow with $\dot x\in F(x+e)$ while $x+e\in C$; jump with $x^+\in G(x+e)$ when $x+e\in D$. The perturbation enters every occurrence of the state.
- **Definition 4.5** (p. 76). An *admissible state perturbation* $e$ has a hybrid time domain as its domain and is measurable in $t$ on each interval $\{t:(t,j)\in\mathrm{dom}\,e\}$. A purely time-dependent measurable signal $e'(t)$ is lifted to any hybrid time domain by ignoring $j$ (equation (4.2)).
- **Definition 4.6** (p. 76–77). A hybrid arc $\varphi$ solves $\mathcal H_e$ if $\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi=\mathrm{dom}\,e$, $\varphi(0,0)+e(0,0)\in C\cup D$, and: (S1e) on each flow interval $I^j$ with nonempty interior, $\varphi+e\in C$ on the interior and $\dot\varphi\in F(\varphi+e)$ almost everywhere; (S2e) at each jump, $\varphi(t,j)+e(t,j)\in D$ and $\varphi(t,j+1)\in G(\varphi(t,j)+e(t,j))$. The authors also say a time signal $e'$ "leads to" a solution, or to nonexistence, in the obvious sense.
- Discussion (p. 77): even with continuous maps, non-closed $C$ or $D$ makes the system fragile; in particular perturbed solutions can "miss" a jump set and flow for ever, which matters when stabilisation relies on jumps.
- **Example 4.7** (p. 77–78). In $\mathbb R^2$, $D$ is a line at unit distance from the origin, $C$ its complement, flow is constant unit speed along the diagonal, and jumps reset to $0$. Unperturbed, the solution from $0$ is periodic (flow to $D$, reset). A perturbation that is non-zero at the single instant the arc reaches $D$ makes the arc skip the jump and flow off to infinity, unlike any unperturbed solution from nearby points.
- **Example 4.8** (p. 77–78). $C$ and $D$ are disjoint dense subsets of $\mathbb R$, $f\equiv c\neq 0$, and $g$ maps into $\mathbb R\setminus D$. Without perturbation no flow is possible (a continuous arc staying in a set with dense complement must be constant), so maximal solutions consist of a single jump. With arbitrarily small (even Lipschitz) perturbations one can produce solutions on any prescribed hybrid time domain, e.g. pure flow $\xi+ct$.
- **Example 4.9** (p. 78–79). $C=(-\infty,1]$ with $\dot x=-x$; $D=(1,\infty)$ with $g\equiv 1$. The origin is globally uniformly asymptotically stable. A constant perturbation $\varepsilon>0$ from $\xi=1$ puts $x+e$ in $D$ for ever, giving the discrete solution that stays at $1$: arbitrarily small perturbations destroy convergence, so the stability is not robust.
- Discussion (p. 78–79): perturbations can also cause *nonexistence*. If $\xi$ is on the boundary of $C\cup D$, a constant small offset pushing $\xi$ outside $C\cup D$ leaves no nontrivial solution. If $\xi$ lies on the boundaries of both $C$ and $D$, a perturbation that takes one value at $t=0$ (pushing out of $D$) and another afterwards (pushing out of $C$) also leaves no solution, even with closed sets and continuous maps.
- **Proposition 4.10** (p. 79). Existence under perturbation. Let $\xi\in C\cup D$. If for some $\delta>0$ either $\xi+\delta\mathbb B\subset D$, or (VCe) $\xi+\delta\mathbb B\subset C$ and every measurable $e'$ bounded by $\delta$ admits a short absolutely continuous solution of $\dot z\in F(z+e'(t))$, then for every admissible perturbation bounded by $\delta$ there is a nontrivial solution of $\mathcal H_e$ from $\xi$. In words: robust existence needs $\xi$ to lie robustly inside the jump set, or robustly inside the flow set with perturbed flow possible.

## 4.3 Generalized solutions (p. 79)

The section links vanishing perturbations to a regularisation of the data.

- **Definition 4.11** (p. 80). Two hybrid arcs are *$\varepsilon$-close* if every point $(t,j)$ of either domain has a partner $(s,j)$ in the other domain, with the same jump index, $|t-s|<\varepsilon$, and values within $\varepsilon$. This is a symmetric, graph-based notion, close in spirit to the Hausdorff distance between graphs; it is used only for compact arcs here, and refined in Chapter 5.
- **Definition 4.12** (p. 81). A compact hybrid arc $\varphi$ is a *compact Hermes solution* of $\mathcal H$ if there are compact arcs $\varphi_i$ solving $\mathcal H_{e_i}$ with admissible $e_i$ whose sup-norms tend to $0$, and $\varphi_i$ is eventually $\varepsilon$-close to $\varphi$ for every $\varepsilon>0$. A general hybrid arc is a Hermes solution if every compact truncation of it is a compact Hermes solution.
- **Definition 4.13** (p. 81). A hybrid arc is a *Krasovskii solution* of $\mathcal H$ if it solves the regularised system $\widehat{\mathcal H}$ (4.5) with $\widehat C=\overline C$, $\widehat D=\overline D$, $\widehat F(x)=\bigcap_{\delta>0}\overline{\mathrm{con}}\,F((x+\delta\mathbb B)\cap C)$ on $\widehat C$, and $\widehat G(x)=\bigcap_{\delta>0}\overline{G((x+\delta\mathbb B)\cap D)}$ on $\widehat D$. Sets are closed; the flow map is closed and convexified; the jump map is closed but not convexified.
- Revisiting the examples (p. 81–82): in Example 4.7 only $C$ changes (to all of $\mathbb R^2$), so a new solution flows through $D$, matching the perturbed behaviour; in Example 4.8 both $\widehat C$ and $\widehat D$ become $\mathbb R$ and solutions exist on every domain; in Example 4.9 $\widehat D=[1,\infty)$ and the stuck discrete solution at $1$ appears as a Krasovskii solution.
- **Example 4.14** (p. 82). Bouncing ball (Example 1.1). The regularisation closes $C$ to $\{x_1\ge 0\}$, convexifies the flow map at the origin to the segment between $0$ and $(0,-\gamma)$, and makes the jump set $\{x_1=0,x_2\le 0\}$ with jump map $(0,-\lambda x_2)$. Exactly one new solution appears: the discrete solution resting at the origin and jumping for ever. It is not produced by a state perturbation of the original model, but it is a Hermes solution because it is the $\varepsilon$-close limit of unperturbed solutions from initial points $(\delta,0)$ with $\delta\to 0$; explicit bounds on $\delta$ are given using the Zeno time estimate (2.4).
- **Example 4.15** (p. 82–83). Fireflies (Example 1.2). The regularisation closes the flow set to $[0,1]^n$ and makes each component of the jump map set-valued, $\{0,1\}$, exactly at the threshold $(1+\varepsilon)x_i=1$ (here $\varepsilon$ is the model's coupling parameter, not a perturbation size). Closing $C$ changes nothing; regularising $G$ adds solutions.
- **Example 4.16** (p. 83). For the discontinuous map of Example 3.12 ("uniformly nonuniform attractivity"), the regularised map is $\{g(x),0,x\}$, so every point admits a constant solution. This explains why the convergence there could not be uniform.
- **Theorem 4.17** (p. 83). If $F$ and $G$ are locally bounded, a hybrid arc is a Hermes solution of $\mathcal H$ if and only if it is a Krasovskii solution. Hermes ⇒ Krasovskii is proved later as Corollary 6.32 (via well-posedness of the regularised system); Krasovskii ⇒ Hermes is Corollary 4.23.
- **Example 4.18** (p. 83–84). Counting zero crossings of a harmonic oscillator with a counter that increments on the "thin" jump set $\{x_2=0\}$ is fragile: perturbations can make the arc miss the set and miss counts. Adding a memory variable $q\in\{-1,1\}$ recording the side of the boundary, with flow allowed only while $x_2 q\ge 0$ and a jump (toggling $q$, incrementing the count) required once the boundary is crossed, gives a system with closed sets and continuous maps that equals its own regularisation. Hence perturbations cannot alter the count. This is presented as a model of the zero-crossing detection used by commercial simulators.

## 4.4 Measurement noise in feedback control (p. 84)

In control loops, measurement noise usually enters only through the feedback, not every occurrence of the state.

- **Example 4.19** (p. 84–87). Plant $\dot z=u$, $|u|\le 1$, target $\mathcal A=\{0,6\}$. The discontinuous feedback that drives to $0$ for $z\le 3$ and to $6$ for $z>3$ makes $\mathcal A$ asymptotically stable, but noise of size $\varepsilon$ oscillating at $z=3$ traps the state in a band around $3$; in the limit $z\equiv 3$ is a Krasovskii (equivalently Hermes) solution, since the regularised map at $3$ is $[-1,1]$. A hysteresis-based hybrid controller with logic variable $q\in\{1,2\}$ (steer to $0$ while $q=1$ and $z\le 4$; steer to $6$ while $q=2$ and $z\ge 2$; otherwise toggle $q$) gives unique solutions that jump at most once and makes $\mathcal A\times\{1,2\}$ globally uniformly asymptotically stable. Under noise affecting $z$ only, system (4.6), noise bounded by $\varepsilon<2$ leaves solutions converging to $\varepsilon$-neighbourhoods of $\{0,6\}$, i.e. practical stability; the chattering equilibrium at $3$ is impossible. But existence can fail at $(4,1)$ with noise $-\varepsilon$ at $t=0$ and $+\varepsilon$ afterwards. Making the flow and jump sets overlap (the flow set enlarged to $(-\infty,5]\times\{1\}\cup[1,\infty)\times\{2\}$) restores robust existence for $\varepsilon<1$, at the cost of non-uniqueness, without harming stability or its robustness, in line with Proposition 4.10.
- **Systems (4.7)–(4.8)** (p. 87). A general closed loop flows by $F(x,\kappa_c(x))$ and jumps by $G(x,\kappa_d(x))$, with regular $F,G$ and possibly discontinuous feedbacks $\kappa_c,\kappa_d$. Noise enters through the feedbacks and the set-membership tests. *Control Hermes solutions* (limits under vanishing measurement noise) and *control Krasovskii solutions* can be defined analogously, and an equivalence like Theorem 4.17 holds (stated without proof, p. 88). A remark notes that convexifying only through the feedback values leads, under continuity of $F$ and local boundedness of $\kappa_c$, back to Krasovskii solutions.

## 4.5 Krasovskii solutions are Hermes solutions (p. 88)

This section proves the approximation direction. Its core is constructive: any solution of the regularised flow can be shadowed by a genuine solution driven by a small, piecewise-constant perturbation.

- **Lemma 4.20** (p. 88). Any Lipschitz arc $z$ on $[a,b]$ can be approximated within $\varepsilon$ in sup-norm by a Lipschitz arc $w$ that is piecewise affine on finitely many disjoint subintervals of length at most $\varepsilon$ covering $[a,b]$, where on each subinterval the slope of $w$ equals $\dot z(\eta)$ at some interior sample point $\eta$. (Proof by a covering argument over points of differentiability.)
- **Theorem 4.21** (p. 90). Let $z$ on $[a,b]$ satisfy $z(t)\in\overline C$ on $(a,b)$ and $\dot z\in\widehat F(z)$ a.e., with $F$ locally bounded. For any $\varepsilon>0$ and any starting point $\psi_a\in C$ within $\varepsilon$ of $z(a)$, there exist a Lipschitz, piecewise linear $\psi$ with $\psi(a)=\psi_a$ and a measurable perturbation $e$ with $|e|\le\varepsilon$ such that $\psi+e\in C$ throughout, $\dot\psi\in F(\psi+e)$ a.e., and $\psi$ stays within $\varepsilon$ of $z$. The construction uses Lemma 4.20, writes each sampled velocity as a convex combination of $n+1$ actual values of $F$ at nearby points of $C$ (Carathéodory), and then splits each short subinterval in proportion to the weights, flowing with each pure velocity in turn; the perturbation is chosen so that $\psi+e$ sits exactly at the corresponding sample point, so $\psi+e$ is piecewise constant.
- **Corollary 4.22** (p. 92). For $F$ locally bounded and a compact Krasovskii solution $\varphi$ of $\mathcal H$, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a hybrid arc $y$ on the *same* domain and an admissible perturbation $e$, both within $\varepsilon$ in sup-norm (4.11), with $y$ solving $\mathcal H_e$. Proved by induction on the number of jumps, using Theorem 4.21 on each flow interval and choosing the perturbation at each jump to land in $D$ with a nearby $G$-value.
- **Corollary 4.23** (p. 93). With $F$ and $G$ locally bounded, every Krasovskii solution is a Hermes solution. (Since the domain is preserved, the approximation is even stronger than $\varepsilon$-closeness.)
- **Corollary 4.24** (p. 93–94). Switching and inclusions. Given a family $f_s$, $s$ in a nonempty subset $S$ of a unit sphere, locally Lipschitz uniformly in $s$, with $\Phi(x)=\mathrm{con}\bigcup_s f_s(x)$ locally bounded, every solution of $\dot z\in\Phi(z)$ on $[a,b]$ is approximated within any $\varepsilon$ by a solution of $\dot\psi=f_{s(t)}(\psi)$ under some piecewise constant switching signal $s(\cdot)$. So solutions under arbitrary switching are dense in the solutions of the convexified inclusion. The proof encodes the switching index as an extra state, applies Theorem 4.21 and a Gronwall estimate.

## 4.6 Notes (p. 94)

Sources: Filippov's book for discontinuous right-hand sides; Hermes and Krasovskii for the two solution notions; Filippov's own notion, which ignores the vector field on measure-zero sets, is unsuitable for hybrid or constrained systems. Theorem 4.3 is due to Hermes and Hájek; the proof route via Lemma 4.20 and Theorem 4.21 is from Sanfelice et al.

## Key ideas to carry away

- Arbitrarily small state perturbations can make a hybrid system miss jumps, chatter at a switching surface, gain wildly many solutions, or lose solutions altogether; these effects are tied to non-closed sets and discontinuous maps.
- Asymptotic stability of a system with irregular data need not survive any perturbation (Examples 4.9, 4.19).
- The Krasovskii regularisation (close $C$ and $D$; close and convexify $F$; close $G$) is exactly the set of behaviours that vanishing perturbations can produce: Hermes = Krasovskii under local boundedness (Theorem 4.17).
- Closeness of hybrid arcs must allow small time shifts within the same jump index (Definition 4.11), because jump times move under perturbation.
- Robust existence of solutions under perturbation needs overlap between flow and jump regions near the boundary (Proposition 4.10); overlap brings non-uniqueness, which is harmless for stability.
- Memory or hysteresis variables (Examples 4.18, 4.19) turn fragile "thin" decision surfaces into closed, overlapping regions whose behaviour is robust to small errors.
- Constructively, solutions of the convexified flow can be realised by fast switching among actual velocities with small piecewise-constant perturbations (Theorem 4.21, Corollary 4.24).

# Chapter 8: Invariance principles (pp. 169–184)

Invariance principles locate where a bounded (precompact) solution must end up without requiring a strict Lyapunov decrease. They combine the weak invariance of $\omega$-limit sets (Proposition 6.21) with either a function that never increases along solutions, or an output that is "small on average" along the single solution being studied. The chapter gives hybrid versions of the Barbashin–Krasovskii–LaSalle principle, uses them to prove pre-asymptotic stability under non-strict Lyapunov conditions, gives single-solution "meagre-limsup" versions, and applies both to switching systems with dwell time. The whole chapter assumes only that $\mathcal{H}$ is **nominally well-posed** (Definition 6.2); the authors stress that full well-posedness plays no role.

Notation: $\Omega(\phi)$ is the $\omega$-limit set of a solution (Definition 6.17). A set $S$ is *weakly forward invariant* if every point of $S$ has at least one complete solution staying in $S$, *weakly backward invariant* if every point of $S$ can be reached, after arbitrarily long hybrid time, by some solution that stayed in $S$, and *weakly invariant* if both (Definition 6.19). "Precompact" means complete and bounded.

## 8.1 Invariance and ω-limits (p. 169)

The section explains why the principles use weak invariance in both directions rather than strong invariance or forward invariance alone.

- **Example 8.1 (weakly invariant sets, p. 169).** A one-dimensional system: flow at unit speed on $[0,2]$; jumps $x\mapsto x+1$ on $[-2,-1]\cup(1,2]$, and from $x=1$ a set-valued jump to $\{-1,2\}$. The largest weakly forward invariant set is $\{-2\}\cup\{-1\}\cup[0,1]$, the largest weakly backward invariant set is $\{-1\}\cup[0,3]$, and the largest weakly invariant set is their intersection $\{-1\}\cup[0,1]$. That set is not strongly invariant, since from each of its points some solution eventually leaves. Every complete solution is eventually the periodic cycle "flow $0\to1$, jump to $-1$, jump to $0$", so the weakly invariant set equals the $\omega$-limit set of every complete solution. It is therefore the tightest convergence target, and using only one direction of invariance would give a larger set.

## 8.2 Invariance principles involving Lyapunov-like functions (p. 170)

For a hybrid arc, $t(j)$ is the first ordinary time at jump index $j$ and $j(t)$ the first jump index at ordinary time $t$.

- **Growth bound (8.1) (p. 170).** $V$'s growth along solutions in $U$ is *bounded by* $u_c,u_d:\mathbb{R}^n\to[-\infty,\infty]$ if, for every solution with range in $U$ and any two ordered hybrid times, the change in $V$ is at most the integral of $u_c$ along the flow plus the sum of $u_d$ evaluated just before each intervening jump. If $u_c,u_d\le0$ on $U$, $V$ is nonincreasing.
- **Theorem 8.2 (invariance principle with a nonincreasing function, p. 171).** Let $V$ be continuous, $u_c,u_d\le0$ on $U$, and $V$'s growth bounded by them on $U$. Then every precompact solution with range in $U$ converges, for some level $r\in V(U)$, to the largest weakly invariant subset of
  $$V^{-1}(r)\cap U\cap\big[u_c^{-1}(0)\cup\big(u_d^{-1}(0)\cap G(u_d^{-1}(0))\big)\big] \quad (8.2),$$
  which is nonempty. Reading: limit points either lie where flow dissipates nothing, or are both a point where a jump dissipates nothing and the image of such a point. The proof uses that $V$ is constant on $\Omega(\phi^*)$, weak forward invariance to see what happens just after a limit point, and weak backward invariance to see what led into it.
- **Example 8.3 (planar oscillator with jumps, p. 172).** Rotation on the upper half-plane, the same rotation applied as a jump on the lower half-plane; $V=|x|$ is conserved. With $u_c$, $u_d$ equal to $0$ on $C$, $D$ respectively and $-\infty$ elsewhere, the set in (8.2) is the plane minus the closed fourth quadrant. Backward invariance removes the open third quadrant (it can be reached only by a jump from the open fourth quadrant), and the largest weakly invariant set turns out to be the range of the solution itself. It illustrates how the backward condition sharpens the conclusion.
- **Natural candidates $u_D$ and $u_C$ ((8.5)–(8.7), pp. 172–173).** For locally Lipschitz $V$, its change splits into an integral of the time derivative during flow plus jump increments (8.5). Without any regularity, $u_D(x)=\max_{\xi\in G(x)}V(\xi)-V(x)$ on $D$ (and $-\infty$ off $D$) bounds jump growth (8.6). For $C^1$ $V$, $u_C(x)=\max_{v\in F(x)}\langle\nabla V(x),v\rangle$ on $C$ (and $-\infty$ off $C$) bounds flow growth (8.7).
- **Corollary 8.4 (invariance principle with $u_C$, $u_D$, p. 173).** With $V$ continuous, $C^1$ near $C$, and $u_C,u_D\le0$ on $U$ (8.8), every precompact solution in $U$ converges to the largest weakly invariant subset of the analogue (8.9) of (8.2), with $u_C$, $u_D$ in place of $u_c$, $u_d$.
- **Example 8.5 (bouncing ball, p. 173).** The principle needs nominal well-posedness, so it is applied to the Krasovskii regularisation of the bouncing ball (Example 4.14), which at the origin allows any downward acceleration between $0$ and $-\gamma$. With energy $V=\tfrac12x_2^2+\gamma x_1$, $u_C\equiv0$ on $C$ and $u_D=-\tfrac12(1-\lambda^2)x_2^2-\gamma x_1\le0$ on $D$. A positive energy level contains no nonempty weakly invariant set (jumps from nonzero points lose energy), so $r=0$, and the set (8.9) reduces to the origin. Every precompact solution converges to the origin.
- **Example 8.6 (attitude stabilisation with quaternions, p. 175).** A rigid-body attitude controller with a logic variable $q\in\{-1,1\}$ and hysteresis width $\delta\in(0,1)$: flow while $q\eta\ge-\delta$, jump (set $q=\operatorname{sign}\eta$) when $q\eta\le-\delta$. With $V=\tfrac12\omega^\top J\omega+2c(1-q\eta)$, $u_C=-\omega^\top K\omega$ and $u_D=4cq\eta<0$ on $D$, so $u_D^{-1}(0)=\varnothing$ and only zero-velocity flow points remain. Since $\omega\equiv0$ forces $\epsilon\equiv0$ under the closed loop, the weakly invariant set lies in the desired attitudes $\{\eta=q,\ \epsilon=0,\ \omega=0\}$. All solutions are complete and bounded, so all converge to the desired set. This is a standard example of hysteresis-based switching that avoids chattering.
- **Corollary 8.7 (special time-domain cases, p. 176).** Under Theorem 8.2's hypotheses: (a) if the solution has infinitely many jumps and its flow intervals shrink to zero length (eventually Zeno-like), only the jump part $V^{-1}(r)\cap U\cap u_d^{-1}(0)\cap G(u_d^{-1}(0))$ matters; (b) if all but finitely many flow intervals have length at least some $\tau>0$, only the flow part $V^{-1}(r)\cap U\cap u_c^{-1}(0)$ matters. Uses Corollary 6.22.

## 8.3 Stability analysis using invariance principles (p. 176)

- **Theorem 8.8 (Barbashin–Krasovskii–LaSalle, p. 176).** For compact $\mathcal{A}$: (a) if $G(D)\subset C\cup D$ and there are a neighbourhood $U$ of $\mathcal{A}$ and a continuous $V$, positive definite on $C\cup D$ with respect to $\mathcal{A}$ and $C^1$ near $C$, with $u_C,u_D\le0$ on $U$, then $\mathcal{A}$ is stable. If also (b) for some $r^*>0$ and every $r\in(0,r^*)$ the largest weakly invariant subset of (8.9) is empty, then $\mathcal{A}$ is LpAS. The proof builds small sublevel sets that are strongly forward invariant: outer semicontinuity of $G$ keeps jumps from near $\mathcal{A}$ close to $\mathcal{A}$. Theorem 8.2 then forces convergence to the level $r=0$, which lies within $\mathcal{A}$.
- **Corollary 8.9 (Lyapunov, p. 177).** Under (a) of Theorem 8.8, $\mathcal{A}$ is pAS if any one of the following holds on $U\setminus\mathcal{A}$: (i) $u_C<0$ and $u_D<0$ (strict decrease in both modes); (ii) $u_C<0$, and every complete purely discrete solution in $U$ converges to $\mathcal{A}$; (iii) $u_D<0$, and every complete purely continuous solution in $U$ converges to $\mathcal{A}$. So strict decrease is needed only in one mode, provided solutions that stay entirely in the other mode are handled separately.
- **Example 8.10 (bouncing ball revisited, p. 178).** Case (iii) applies at once: jumps strictly dissipate energy, and the only complete purely continuous solution of the regularised ball is the one resting at the origin. This is shorter than the arguments of Examples 3.19 and 8.5.

## 8.4 Meagre-limsup invariance principles (p. 178)

Theorem 8.2 needs the growth bound for *every* solution in $U$. Here the conditions concern only the one solution under study.

- **Weakly meagre (p. 178).** A measurable $f:\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\to\mathbb{R}$ is weakly meagre if, over any sequence of disjoint closed intervals with lengths bounded below, the infimum of $|f|$ on the $n$-th interval tends to zero. A sufficient condition is that the integral of $|f|$ over a sliding window of fixed length tends to zero; in particular every integrable function is weakly meagre.
- **Theorem 8.11 (meagre-limsup conditions, p. 178).** Let $\phi$ be complete and let $\ell_c,\ell_d:U\to[0,\infty]$ be continuous, with $\operatorname{rge}\phi\subset U$, such that (a) if $\phi$ flows for unbounded time, $t\mapsto\ell_c(\phi(t,j(t)))$ is weakly meagre; and (b) if $\phi$ jumps infinitely often, then in each flow interval $[t(j),t(j+1)]$ some point has $\ell_d$ value tending to zero (limsup over $j$ equal to zero). Then $\Omega(\phi)\subset E_{\phi,\ell_c}\cup E_{\phi,\ell_d}$, the zero sets of $\ell_c$ and $\ell_d$ restricted to the closure of the range. Two variants: (b) may be replaced by a structural condition that $\ell_d$ vanishes wherever any solution has three consecutive hybrid times at the same ordinary time (two jumps in a row); and if all but finitely many flow intervals have length at least $\tau>0$, (b) is unnecessary and $\Omega(\phi)\subset E_{\phi,\ell_c}$. The proof splits the times at which $\phi$ approaches a limit point into those preceded or followed by a flow of fixed length, which contradicts meagreness, and those squeezed between two nearby jumps, which contradicts (b).
- **Remark on discontinuous $\ell$ (p. 180).** The same proof works for discontinuous $\ell_c,\ell_d$ if the zero sets are replaced by the points of the closed range approached by range points along which $\ell$ has liminf zero.
- **Corollary 8.12 (purely continuous or purely discrete cases, p. 180).** (a) If $\phi$ eventually stops jumping and $\ell_c$ along it is weakly meagre, $\Omega(\phi)\subset E_{\phi,\ell_c}$. (b) If $\phi$ has bounded ordinary time (Zeno or eventually discrete) and $\ell_d$ satisfies the limsup condition, $\Omega(\phi)\subset E_{\phi,\ell_d}$. Each follows by taking the other function to be a positive constant.
- **Example 8.13 (bouncing ball, Zeno solution, p. 180).** For a Zeno solution, take $\ell_d(x)=|x_1|$ and the jump instants as the $t^*_j$. Corollary 8.12(b) puts the $\omega$-limit in $\{x_1=0\}$, from which convergence to the origin follows.
- **Lower semicontinuous closure (p. 180).** $\underline\ell(x)=\liminf_{x_i\to x}\ell(x_i)$, the largest lsc minorant of $\ell$.
- **Corollary 8.14 (invariance with a meagre-limsup function, p. 181).** If $\phi$ is precompact and $\ell_c,\ell_d:\mathbb{R}^n\to[0,\infty]$ satisfy the meagre-limsup conditions along it, then $\phi$ converges to the largest weakly invariant subset of $\underline{\ell_c}^{-1}(0)\cup\underline{\ell_d}^{-1}(0)$. With flow intervals uniformly bounded below in length, $\underline{\ell_c}^{-1}(0)$ alone suffices. The difference from Theorem 8.11 is that invariance is now added, which can shrink the target set.

## 8.5 Invariance principles for switching systems (p. 181)

The setting is a switching system $\dot z=f_\sigma(z)$ with finitely many continuous vector fields $f_q$ (from (2.5)), modelled as hybrid systems as in Section 2.4. Hypotheses:

- **(S1) (p. 181):** each mode has a $C^1$ function $V_q$ and a continuous $W_q\ge0$ with $\langle\nabla V_q,f_q\rangle\le-W_q$ everywhere, so $V_q$ is nonincreasing while mode $q$ is active;
- **(S2) (p. 181):** along the solution, at each re-entry into mode $q^*$, the value of $V_{q^*}$ is no larger than it was when that mode was last left. Together with (S1), this makes $V_{q^*}$ nonincreasing over the union of all of mode $q^*$'s active intervals. This is a "multiple Lyapunov functions" condition.

Two proof techniques are shown: (i) splice together a solution's intervals in one mode into a new hybrid arc and apply Section 8.2; (ii) use the $W_q$ as a weakly meagre output and apply Section 8.4.

- **Proposition 8.15 (dwell-time invariance principle, take I, p. 182).** Assume (S1) and that (S2) holds for every dwell-time solution. For a complete, bounded dwell-time solution with dwell time $\tau_D>0$ there are levels $r_q$ such that $z$ approaches the union, over modes active infinitely often, of $M_q(r_q,\tau_D)$. Here $M_q(r_q,\tau_D)$ is the largest subset of $V_q^{-1}(r_q)\cap W_q^{-1}(0)$ in which every point lies on a trajectory of mode $q$ of duration $\tau_D/2$ that stays in the set and starts or ends at that point. The proof concatenates mode-$q^*$ intervals into an auxiliary nominally well-posed hybrid system with a timer and an arbitrary jump within a compact set (8.13), then uses Corollaries 8.4 and 8.7.
- **Proposition 8.16 (dwell-time invariance principle, take II, p. 183).** Assume (S1), and let a complete, bounded dwell-time solution satisfy (S2). Then $z$ approaches the largest subset $N$ of $\bigcup_p W_p^{-1}(0)$ that is invariant in a two-sided sense: each point of $N$ lies at the end of a backward trajectory of some mode $p_1$ and at the start of a forward trajectory of some mode $p_2$, staying in the respective $W^{-1}(0)\cap N$, with total duration at least $\tau_D$. The proof shows that $\ell_c=W_\sigma(z)$ is integrable along the solution (bounded $V_q$ plus (S1), (S2)), hence weakly meagre, and applies Corollary 8.14; the printed proof ends by stating that it remains to check the projected set has the claimed invariance.

## 8.6 Notes (p. 184)

Origins: Barbashin–Krasovskii and LaSalle; integrable-output versions by Byrnes–Martin, extended to inclusions by Ryan and to meagre outputs by Logemann–Ryan; discontinuous systems by Shevitz–Paden and Bacciotti–Ceragioli; hybrid, impulsive and switching results by Lygeros et al., Hespanha and coauthors, Bacciotti–Mazzi and Mancilla-Aguilar–García. The main results are from Sanfelice et al.; the attitude example from Mayhew et al. (reference [90]); the switching applications from Goebel et al.; the $\ell_{q^*}$ idea in Proposition 8.16 resembles a proof of Hespanha et al.

## Key ideas to carry away

- A function that merely never increases is enough to locate limits: precompact solutions approach the largest *weakly invariant* set inside a level set of $V$ where neither flow nor jumps dissipate (Theorem 8.2, Corollary 8.4).
- In the hybrid case, "no dissipation" has a specific two-sided form: a point where flow dissipates nothing, or a non-dissipating jump point that is itself the image of one. Weak *backward* invariance often removes much of the candidate set (Examples 8.1, 8.3).
- For stability, strict decrease is needed in only one of flow and jumps if solutions that never use the other mode are dealt with separately (Corollary 8.9). The bouncing ball is the model case.
- The time-domain structure of the particular solution decides which half of the condition matters: dwell-time-like solutions need only the flow condition, Zeno-like solutions only the jump condition (Corollaries 8.7, 8.12).
- Meagre-limsup principles need a condition only along the solution in hand, for example an output that is integrable over time along it, and no conserved or decreasing function at all (Theorem 8.11, Corollary 8.14).
- Everything requires only nominal well-posedness and precompactness (complete and bounded), not robustness.

# Chapter 2: The solution concept (pp. 25–42)

This chapter makes Chapter 1's informal model rigorous. It defines the data of a hybrid system, introduces hybrid time (pairs $(t, j)$ of elapsed flow time and number of jumps) and the hybrid time domains on which solutions live, and defines a solution as a hybrid arc obeying the flow constraints on intervals of flow and the jump constraints at jumps. It then gives basic existence and uniqueness conditions and a classification of how maximal solutions can end. Finally it shows that classes of switching signals (dwell-time, persistent dwell-time, average dwell-time) can be generated by simple hybrid systems, so that restrictions on switching become part of the data. Everything later in the book (stability, robustness, convergence of solutions) is stated in terms of these objects.

## 2.1 Data of a hybrid system (p. 25)

A set-valued mapping $M : \mathbb{R}^m \rightrightarrows \mathbb{R}^n$ assigns a subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ to each point.

- **Definition 2.1 (Domain of a set-valued mapping, p. 25).** The domain of $M$ is the set of points where $M(x)$ is nonempty.
- **Definition 2.2 (Data of a hybrid system, p. 25).** The data are a flow set $C \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, a set-valued flow map $F$ with $C \subset \operatorname{dom} F$, a jump set $D \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, and a set-valued jump map $G$ with $D \subset \operatorname{dom} G$. The system is written $\mathcal{H} = (C, F, D, G)$.

Single-valued $f$, $g$ defined only on $C$, $D$ fit by extending them with empty values elsewhere. The authors observe that one could fold $C$ and $D$ into the maps (making $F$ empty off $C$ and $G$ empty off $D$), reducing the data to two objects, but they keep all four because the geometry of the flow and jump sets is analysed directly in much of the book.

## 2.2 Hybrid time domains and hybrid arcs (p. 26)

Solutions are indexed by ordinary time $t$ and jump count $j$ together, but only certain subsets of $\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\times\mathbb{N}$ make sense as indices.

- **Definition 2.3 (Hybrid time domains, p. 27).** A *compact hybrid time domain* is a finite union $\bigcup_{j=0}^{J-1} [t_j, t_{j+1}] \times \{j\}$ with $0 = t_0 \le t_1 \le \dots \le t_J$. A *hybrid time domain* is a set $E \subset \mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\times\mathbb{N}$ whose truncation $E \cap ([0,T]\times\{0,\dots,J\})$ is a compact hybrid time domain for every $(T, J) \in E$. Equivalently it is a finite or infinite union of such intervals, the last of which may be half-open $[t_J, T)$ with $T$ finite or infinite. Equal consecutive times are allowed, so several jumps at one instant are possible.

Points of one hybrid time domain are totally ordered: $(t,j)$ precedes $(t',j')$ when $t < t'$, or $t = t'$ and $j \le j'$; within a single domain this is the same as $t + j \le t' + j'$. Points from different domains need not be comparable (e.g. $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$). The quantities $\sup_t E$, $\sup_j E$, $\sup E = (\sup_t E, \sup_j E)$ and $\mathrm{length}(E) = \sup_t E + \sup_j E$ are defined.

- **Definition 2.4 (Hybrid arc, p. 28).** A function $\phi$ on a hybrid time domain is a hybrid arc if, for each $j$, $t \mapsto \phi(t,j)$ is locally absolutely continuous on $I^j = \{t : (t,j) \in \operatorname{dom}\phi\}$. This matters only for intervals with nonempty interior; on those, $\dot\phi(t,j)$ exists almost everywhere. The authors stress a set-valued reading: the arc determines its own domain, so one does not fix a domain and then seek a solution on it. This view later lets set-convergence ideas apply to hybrid arcs (Chapters 4 and 5).
- **Definition 2.5 (Types of hybrid arcs, p. 28).** By the shape of the domain, an arc is: *nontrivial* (at least two points); *complete* (unbounded domain, i.e. infinite length); *Zeno* (complete with finite $\sup_t$); *eventually discrete* (finite $\sup_t = T$, with at least two domain points at $t = T$); *discrete* (nontrivial and all at $t = 0$); *eventually continuous* (finite $\sup_j = J$, with at least two domain points at $j = J$); *continuous* (nontrivial and all at $j = 0$); *compact* (compact domain). Completeness implies neither unbounded time nor unbounded jumps. Every discrete arc is eventually discrete; complete and eventually discrete implies Zeno, but Zeno is much broader. Informally, Zeno arcs that are not eventually discrete are "genuinely Zeno", complete discrete arcs "instantaneously Zeno".

## 2.3 Solutions and their basic properties (p. 29)

- **Definition 2.6 (Solution to a hybrid system, p. 29).** A hybrid arc $\phi$ is a solution to $(C, F, D, G)$ if $\phi(0,0) \in C \cup D$ and (S1) on every flow interval $I^j$ with nonempty interior, $\phi(t,j) \in C$ for $t$ in the interior of $I^j$ and $\dot\phi(t,j) \in F(\phi(t,j))$ for almost every $t$; (S2) whenever both $(t,j)$ and $(t,j+1)$ are in the domain, $\phi(t,j) \in D$ and $\phi(t,j+1) \in G(\phi(t,j))$. The definition is deliberately permissive: endpoints of flow intervals need not lie in $C$, and flowing through points of $D$ is not forbidden.
- **Definition 2.7 (Maximal solutions, p. 30).** A solution is maximal if no other solution extends it to a strictly larger domain. Complete solutions are maximal; the converse fails.
- **Example 2.8 (Solutions, p. 31).** A planar system with constant flow $f = (1,1)$ on the complement of a jump set lying between the $x_1$-axis and a downward-sloping line that meets the axis at $x_1 = 2$, and a linear jump map depending only on $x_1$, $g(x) = (\tfrac34 x_1, \tfrac14 x_1)$ (as far as the extracted text can be read), which can send points back onto the boundary of the jump set. From different initial points the maximal solutions are respectively continuous, eventually continuous, eventually discrete (flow, then infinitely many jumps at one instant), discrete (the origin, which jumps to itself forever despite $f$ being nonzero there), and Zeno with jump gaps halving each time. All are complete.
- **Example 2.9 (Solutions, p. 33).** On $\mathbb{R}$, with $C = \{|x| \ge 1\}$, $f(x) = x^2$, $D = [0,1]$, $g(x) = x - 1$: from $x = 1$ one maximal solution escapes to infinity in finite time, another jumps twice to $-1$ and stops; from $-2$ the solution flows to $-1$ and cannot continue. All three are maximal but not complete.

$\mathcal{S}_\mathcal{H}(S)$ denotes the set of maximal solutions starting in $S$.

- **Proposition 2.10 (Basic existence, p. 33).** Let $\xi \in C \cup D$. If $\xi \in D$, or if the *viability condition* (VC) holds at $\xi$ (some absolutely continuous solution of $\dot z \in F(z)$ starts at $\xi$ and stays in $C$ for a short positive time), then a nontrivial solution exists from $\xi$. If (VC) holds at every point of $C \setminus D$, nontrivial solutions exist from every point of $C \cup D$, and each maximal solution is of exactly one of three kinds: (a) complete; (b) bounded domain ending in a flow interval that is open on the right and cannot be extended by any constrained flow (e.g. finite escape); (c) bounded domain ending at a point outside $C \cup D$. If $G(D) \subset C \cup D$, case (c) is excluded. The authors then check Examples 2.8 and 2.9 against this: in 2.8 everything is complete; in 2.9 (VC) fails at $-1$, which is why one solution fits none of the three cases, while the others illustrate (b) and (c).
- **Proposition 2.11 (Basic uniqueness, p. 34).** Every point of $C \cup D$ has a unique maximal solution if (a) from any point of $C \setminus D$ any two constrained flows coincide; (b) from no point of $C \cap D$ is a constrained flow possible (so there is no choice between flowing and jumping); (c) $G$ is single-valued on $D$.
- **Example 2.12 (Bouncing ball, solutions, p. 35).** For Example 1.1, existence holds via (VC) (with a reference to Lemma 5.26 for points on the surface moving upward, and the trivial resting solution from the origin). Maximal solutions are complete: (c) is excluded since $g(D) \subset C$, and (b) because solutions are bounded (by an energy argument, Example 3.19). Every maximal solution from a nonzero point is Zeno: the first impact time is given by (2.3), and summing the geometric series of bounce durations gives a finite total flow time (2.4) whenever $\lambda < 1$.

## 2.4 Generators for classes of switching signals (p. 35)

A switching system $\dot z = f_\sigma(z)$ ((2.5)), with $Q$ finite and each $f_q$ continuous, is usually solved with a right-continuous, piecewise-constant signal $\sigma$. If the signal's discontinuities do not accumulate (except possibly at the end), a solution corresponds to a solution of the crude hybrid model (1.13); if they accumulate earlier, hybrid time domains cannot record the mode. Conversely, (1.13) has many solutions (all those with several jumps at once, including discrete ones) that are not switching-system solutions.

Signal classes are defined via the switching times $t_1, t_2, \dots$:
- *dwell-time* $\tau_D$: consecutive switches are at least $\tau_D$ apart;
- *persistent dwell-time* $\tau_D$ with period $T$: there are switch-free intervals of length at least $\tau_D$, and no more than $T$ elapses between the end of one such interval and the start of the next;
- *weak dwell-time*: infinitely many switch-free intervals of length $\tau_D$;
- *average dwell-time* $\tau_D$ with offset (chatter bound) $N_\circ$: the number of switches in $[s, t]$ is at most $(t-s)/\tau_D + N_\circ$. With $N_\circ = 1$ this coincides with the dwell-time class.

The point is that the class can be built into the data of a hybrid system.

- **Example 2.13 (Dwell-time signals, p. 38).** Augment with a timer $\tau \in [0, \tau_D]$ that flows at any rate in $[0,1]$; a switch to any $q \in Q$ is allowed only when $\tau = \tau_D$, and resets $\tau$ to 0 ((2.6)). Dwell-time solutions with infinitely many switches correspond to solutions of (2.6) and conversely (the explicit correspondence uses $\tau = \min\{t - t_j, \tau_D\}$).
- **Example 2.14 (Persistent dwell time, p. 39).** Periods of arbitrary switching are modelled by the differential inclusion $\dot z \in \Phi(z)$, where $\Phi(z)$ is the closed convex hull of $\{f_q(z)\}$; every switched trajectory solves this inclusion, and by Corollary 4.24 every solution of the inclusion can be approximated by switching. The hybrid model (2.7) adds a mode value $q = 0$ for "arbitrary switching" and two timers: $\tau_1$ measures quiet intervals in a fixed mode (up to $\tau_D$), $\tau_2$ measures arbitrary-switching intervals (up to $T$). Persistent dwell-time solutions correspond to solutions of (2.7) started with both timers at 0 and $\tau_1$ growing at rate 1; conversely the $z$-component of such a solution can be approximated arbitrarily well on compact time intervals by persistent dwell-time solutions.
- **Example 2.15 (Average dwell-time, p. 40).** The time domain of an average dwell-time solution satisfies $j - i \le (t - s)/\tau_D + N_\circ$ for ordered domain points (2.8). Such domains are produced exactly by the one-dimensional system (2.9): a token count $\tau \in [0, N_\circ]$ refills at any rate in $[0, 1/\tau_D]$, and each jump spends one token (allowed when $\tau \ge 1$). Every solution's domain satisfies (2.8), and every domain satisfying (2.8) is the domain of a solution started at $\tau = N_\circ$. Attaching modes gives (2.10), whose solutions started at $N_\circ$ with the counter refilling at the full rate correspond one-to-one with average dwell-time switching solutions.

## 2.5 Notes (p. 41)

Earlier work parameterised hybrid solutions by real time alone, as piecewise continuous functions with left and right limits; this remains common for impulsive and switching systems and for measure-driven and complementarity models. The need for a richer parameterisation was recognised in the theses of Deshpande and Lygeros via sequences of intervals with non-strict inequalities ("hybrid time trajectories"); a hybrid time domain is equivalent, but as a subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ it is easier to analyse. Hybrid time domains first appeared in Goebel et al. and, as "hybrid time sets", in Collins. Definition 2.6 matches "runs"/"executions" of hybrid automata and, for closed $C$, the earlier definition in Goebel et al. Dynamic equations on time scales are contrasted: there the time set is fixed in advance. Switching-signal classes follow Hespanha.

## Key ideas to carry away

- Hybrid time is two-dimensional: a solution is indexed by $(t, j)$, and its domain is a staircase of intervals, possibly with zero-length steps (several jumps at one instant).
- The domain belongs to the solution: different solutions, even from the same point, can have entirely different domains.
- The solution definition only constrains interiors of flow intervals to $C$ and jump origins to $D$; overlap of $C$ and $D$ gives nondeterminism.
- Maximal solutions either are complete, stop because flow cannot continue (e.g. blow-up), or stop by jumping out of $C \cup D$; the last is ruled out by $G(D) \subset C \cup D$.
- Completeness and Zeno behaviour are properties of the domain; a solution can be complete with bounded time (Zeno) or bounded jumps.
- Uniqueness needs no choice between two flows, between flowing and jumping, or between jump values.
- Restrictions on how often events may happen (dwell time, average dwell time) can be generated by small timer or token-counter subsystems, making them part of the model rather than an external assumption.

## Candidate uses for a discrete-event computing pipeline

*Speculative; for the team lead to evaluate.*

- A per-block trace with timestamps and causal sequence numbers is essentially a hybrid time domain: the timestamp is $t$ and the global sequence number is $j$; events sharing a timestamp are zero-length steps. The lexicographic order on $(t, j)$ matches the trace's causal order within one run.
- The average dwell-time generator (2.9) is a token bucket; checking each trace against $j - i \le (t-s)/\tau_D + N_\circ$ for each event type would give empirical burst and rate bounds (e.g. issues per unit time) in a form the stability results can use.
- The three ways a maximal solution can end (Proposition 2.10) give a checklist for a pipeline model: does it run forever, stall because no flow or event is allowed (deadlock, e.g. window full and no worker ready), or step outside its invariant set (a conservation law violated)?
- Proposition 2.11's three conditions are a way to state when the model is deterministic: no simultaneous choice between "wait" and "fire", and no choice of which event fires. Where the real scheduler has such choices, set-valued $G$ represents them honestly.
- Zeno-type behaviour (many jumps with little elapsed time) is the formal name for event bursts; whether the model admits it is decidable from the data.

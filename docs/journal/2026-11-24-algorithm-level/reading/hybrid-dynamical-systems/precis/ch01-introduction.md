# Chapter 1: Introduction (pp. 1–23)

The chapter sets out, informally, the single modelling format used throughout the book: a state that may evolve continuously while it lies in a flow set and may change instantaneously while it lies in a jump set. It gives no definitions of solutions (those wait for Chapter 2); its job is to show by example that impact mechanics, biological synchronisation, power electronics and many hybrid control schemes all fit this format. It then shows how models written in other hybrid frameworks (explicit discrete modes, hybrid automata, impulsive differential equations, switching systems) translate into it, so that the stability theory of later chapters applies to all of them. Several examples introduced here (the bouncing ball, sample-and-hold control, uniting local and global controllers) return repeatedly later in the book.

## 1.1 The modelling framework (p. 1)

A hybrid system is written as a pair of constrained dynamics on $\mathbb{R}^n$: a differential inclusion $\dot x \in F(x)$ allowed when $x \in C$, and a difference inclusion $x^+ \in G(x)$ allowed when $x \in D$ (display (1.1)). A reader may prefer the single-valued version with $\dot x = f(x)$ and $x^+ = g(x)$ (display (1.2)). Continuous evolution is called *flow*, instantaneous change is called a *jump*, and the four data are named the flow set $C$, flow map $F$ (or $f$), jump set $D$ and jump map $G$ (or $g$). Consistency requires $f$ to be defined at least on $C$ and $g$ at least on $D$; in the set-valued case $F$ and $G$ must have nonempty values on $C$ and $D$ respectively. A purely continuous-time system is the special case $C = \mathbb{R}^n$, $D = \emptyset$; a purely discrete-time system is $C = \emptyset$, $D = \mathbb{R}^n$.

## 1.2 Examples in science and engineering (p. 2)

Mechanical systems with impacts are the motivating class: between impacts they flow, and if impacts are idealised as instantaneous they become jumps.

- **Example 1.1 (Bouncing ball, p. 2).** State $x = (x_1, x_2)$, height and vertical velocity. Flow is allowed when the ball is above the surface, or on it with nonnegative velocity. During flow the velocity decreases at the gravitational rate $\gamma$; the authors set $f(0) = 0$, accepting that $f$ is then discontinuous at the origin. Jumps happen on the surface with negative velocity, $D = \{x_1 = 0, x_2 < 0\}$, and reverse and shrink the velocity by a restitution factor $\lambda \in (0,1)$: $g(x) = (0, -\lambda x_2)$. The authors note that $-\lambda x$ would serve equally, since it agrees with $g$ on $D$. In this model a jump is always followed by flow; by contrast, a Newton's cradle modelled as a chain of pairwise collisions needs several consecutive jumps.
- **Example 1.2 (Flashing fireflies, p. 4).** Each of $n$ fireflies has an internal clock $x_i \in [0,1]$ that rises during flow at a positive continuous rate $f_i(x_i)$. The flow set is $[0,1)^n$; a jump occurs when some clock hits the threshold 1. At a jump every other clock is multiplied by $(1+\varepsilon)$, but any clock that would thereby reach or pass 1 is reset to 0 together with the flashing one. Simulations show the clocks synchronising for $n = 2$ and $n = 10$ (Figure 1.2).
- **Example 1.3 (Power control with a thyristor, p. 4).** A load (resistor and inductor) is fed from a sinusoidal source (itself generated by a harmonic oscillator, (1.3)) through a thyristor whose gate fires at a programmed angle $\alpha$. The state is six-dimensional: the oscillator, the load current $i_L$, the capacitor voltage, a logic variable $q \in \{0,1\}$ (off/on) and a timer $\tau$. During flow $q$ is constant and $\tau$ counts elapsed time. Switching on requires the thyristor to be off with zero current, the timer past $\alpha/\omega$ and positive capacitor voltage; switching off happens when the thyristor is on and the current reaches zero while falling. The jump map toggles $q$ and resets $\tau$.

## 1.3 Control system examples (p. 7)

Continuous state feedback runs into practical and theoretical limits: the state may only be known intermittently, the loop may be very sensitive to measurement error, or no single feedback law may meet the goal. Hybrid control addresses each.

- **Example 1.4 (Sample-and-hold control, p. 7).** A plant $\dot z = \tilde f(z,u)$ with feedback $u = \kappa(z)$ is implemented digitally: the state is sampled every $T$ seconds and the control is held constant in between. The closed loop has state $(z, u, \tau)$. It flows while $\tau \in [0, T)$ with $\dot u = 0$ and $\dot\tau = 1$ ((1.5)); it jumps when $\tau = T$, setting $u^+ = \kappa(z)$ and $\tau^+ = 0$ and leaving $z$ unchanged ((1.6)).
- **Example 1.5 (A quantised control system, p. 9).** The scalar plant $\dot\zeta = \zeta + u$ is measured through a quantiser, $y = \mu q(\zeta/\mu)$, with an adjustable zoom $\mu > 0$. The quantiser $q$ is assumed to satisfy two conditions with constants $\Delta \ll M$: within range $M$ the quantisation error is at most $\Delta$, and a small quantised value guarantees the argument is within range. The feedback is $u = -ky$ with $k > 1$. Flow is allowed while $|q(\zeta/\mu)|$ lies in a band $[\ell_{in}, \ell_{out}]$; below the band $\mu$ is shrunk by a factor $\lambda_{in} < 1$, above it $\mu$ is enlarged by $\lambda_{out} > 1$. Several consecutive jumps may be needed to reach the band. If the thresholds are chosen well, the system eventually only ever hits the lower jump set, $\mu$ shrinks geometrically, and $\zeta$ goes to zero with it.
- **Example 1.6 (Reset linear control systems, p. 10).** A reset controller has its state reset when a condition on its input and output holds; the Clegg integrator, for instance, resets its output to zero whenever input and output differ in sign (Figure 1.5). For a linear plant and controller the closed loop flows as $\dot x = A_f x$ and resets as $x^+ = A_g x$, with the jump set given by a quadratic inequality $x^\top M x \le 0$ for symmetric $M$ and the flow set its complement. Since the origin then lies in $D$ but not in $C$ and $g(0) = 0$, the origin admits only jumps forever. "Temporal regularisation" cures this: a timer $\tau$ is added, jumps require $\tau \ge \delta$ and reset $\tau$ to 0, and flow is also allowed while $\tau \le \delta$; the timer's rate is set to zero once it reaches $2\delta$, keeping it bounded.
- **Example 1.7 (Combining local and global controllers, p. 12).** Some tasks (for example robust global stabilisation of an inverted multi-link pendulum actuated on one link) cannot be achieved by any continuous feedback because of topological obstructions, but can be by switching between controllers. Given a local law $\kappa_1$ with good performance near the origin and a global law $\kappa_2$, a logic variable $q \in \{1,2\}$ selects the active law. Hysteresis prevents chattering: with sets $C_i, D_i$ (Figure 1.9), law $q$ stays in force while $z \in C_q$ and is toggled ($q^+ = 3 - q$) when $z \in D_q$ ((1.8)). For this to work, solutions under $\kappa_1$ that start in $D_2$ must stay in a closed strict subset of $C_1$, and solutions under $\kappa_1$ that remain in $C_1$ must converge; both follow from picking $C_1$ a small neighbourhood of the origin and $D_2$ a smaller neighbourhood inside it. The construction is illustrated on mode-switching head-positioning control in hard disk drives (double-integrator plant), with $C_2 = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus D_2$ and $D_1 = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus C_1$.

## 1.4 Connections to other modelling frameworks (p. 15)

Translating other frameworks into (1.1)/(1.2) lets the book's stability theory, including the invariance principles of Section 8.5, be applied to them.

### 1.4.1 Systems with explicit discrete states or logical modes (p. 15)

Many hybrid systems have a continuous state $z$ and a discrete mode $q$ taking values in a finite set $Q$. The mode changes only by jumps; the continuous state flows and may or may not also jump (a thermostat's temperature does not jump; a circuit current might jump when a switch opens). Such a system is specified by, for each mode $q$, a flow set $C_q$, flow map $F_q$, jump set $D_q$ and jump map $G_q$ into $Q \times \mathbb{R}^n$ ((1.9)). It is put in standard form by taking $x = (q, z)$, $C = \bigcup_q \{q\} \times C_q$, $F(x) = (0, F_q(z))$, $D = \bigcup_q \{q\}\times D_q$, $G(x) = G_q(z)$ ((1.10)).

- **Example 1.8 (p. 16)** rewrites Example 1.7 in this form, with $F_q(z) = \tilde f(z, \kappa_q(z))$ and $G_q(z) = (3-q, z)$.
- **Example 1.9 (Thermostat, p. 16).** Room temperature obeys $\dot z = -z + z_0 + z_\Delta q$ ((1.11)), with heater state $q \in \{0,1\}$ and $z_0 < z_{\min} < z_{\max} < z_0 + z_\Delta$. With thresholds 70 and 80, the heater is switched off at or above 80 and on at or below 70. In the form (1.10): $C_0 = \{z > 70\}$, $D_0 = \{z \le 70\}$, $C_1 = \{z < 80\}$, $D_1 = \{z \ge 80\}$, and the jump map toggles $q$ to $1 - q$.

### 1.4.2 Hybrid automata (p. 17)

A hybrid automaton is given by modes $Q$, a domain $\mathrm{Domain}(q)$ for each mode, a flow map $f(q, \cdot)$, a set of edges $\mathrm{Edges} \subset Q \times Q$, a guard $\mathrm{Guard}(q,q')$ for each edge, and a reset map $\mathrm{Reset}(q, q', \cdot)$ for each edge. It is converted via (1.12): $C_q$ is the domain, $D_q$ is the union of the guards leaving $q$, and $G_q(z)$ collects, over all edges whose guard contains $z$, the pair (target mode, reset value). Overlapping guards therefore make the jump map set-valued even when all resets are identities.

- **Example 1.10 (Modelling a hybrid automaton, p. 18).** A two-mode automaton on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with domains $\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\times\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}$, a constant flow in mode 1 and a rotation in mode 2, three edges (a self-loop on mode 1 and edges each way), and guards that overlap at the origin of mode 1. The conversion yields a set-valued $G_1$ (at $z = 0$ both the reset to $(-1,0)$ and the switch to mode 2 are possible), and finally a hybrid system on $\mathbb{R}^3$ with explicit $C$, $F$, $D$, $G$ (Figure 1.12).

### 1.4.3 Impulsive differential equations (p. 20)

An ODE $\dot z = f(z)$ subject to impulses $\Delta z(t_i) = g(z, t_i)$ at prescribed increasing, unbounded times $t_i$ can be modelled by adding a clock $\tau$, flowing when $\tau \notin T = \{t_i\}$ and jumping when $\tau \in T$. The authors warn (pointing to Chapter 4) that this direct formulation is not robust to perturbations. A preferred formulation carries two clocks: $\tau_1$ records time, and $\tau_2$ counts down to the next impulse, so that jumps are triggered by $\tau_2 = 0$ and reset $\tau_2$ to the gap $t_{i+1} - t_i$; this robustly prevents flow at impulse times.

### 1.4.4 Switching systems (p. 21)

A switching system is a family $\dot z = f_q(z)$, $q \in Q$, with a switching signal choosing the active vector field. The crude hybrid model (1.13) allows flow with $q$ fixed and arbitrary jumps of $q$ anywhere, $C = D = \mathbb{R}^n \times Q$. The authors say this is of little practical use: for a given signal the system is just a time-varying ODE, and analysis under all signals is better done with differential inclusions (cf. Corollary 4.24). The hybrid approach earns its keep when signals are restricted to a class, for example when switching frequency is limited, which can be encoded with a clock; this is developed in Section 2.4.

## 1.5 Notes (p. 22)

The flow-set/flow-map/jump-set/jump-map format originates with Goebel et al. and Goebel and Teel; related set-valued formulations appear in work of Aubin and coauthors and Collins. Earlier hybrid-automaton and discrete/continuous-state models (Witsenhausen, Tavernini, Alur et al., Henzinger, Branicky, Lygeros, van der Schaft and Schumacher) are cited, with references for impulsive equations and switching systems (Liberzon). Alternative treatments of impacts and friction via complementarity systems and measure-driven inclusions are mentioned. Sources for the individual examples are given (Strogatz–Mirollo for fireflies, Liberzon for the quantiser, Prieur for uniting controllers, and hard disk drive literature).

## Key ideas to carry away

- A hybrid system is four objects: where flow is allowed ($C$), how the state flows ($F$), where jumps are allowed ($D$), and how the state jumps ($G$).
- Flow and jump sets act as constraints, not triggers; when they overlap, both behaviours are possible, and set-valued maps capture genuine nondeterminism.
- Discrete modes, timers and counters are just extra state components; a mode is constant during flow and a timer has rate 1.
- Timers make periodic or time-triggered events (sampling, impulses, firing angles) state-dependent, which is what allows a uniform treatment.
- Hysteresis (separating $C_q$ from $D_q$) and temporal regularisation (forcing a minimum flow time between jumps) are standard devices to rule out pathological rapid switching.
- Formulations that look equivalent can differ in robustness: a clock that must hit an exact set of times is fragile; a count-down timer is robust.
- Hybrid automata, impulsive equations and switching systems all embed in the format, so one stability theory serves them all.

## Candidate uses for a discrete-event computing pipeline

*Speculative; for the team lead to evaluate.*

- The scanner–workers–reader pipeline can be written in exactly this format: continuous state is elapsed time and per-worker progress timers (flow), and each traced event (issue, enqueue, start, ready, taken, consumed) is a jump with its own guard (a subset of $D$) and reset.
- The admission rule "issued − taken < W" is naturally a guard on the issue jump; the counts themselves are discrete modes that only change at jumps, like $q$ in Section 1.4.1.
- Several events stamped with the same time map to consecutive jumps, as in the Newton's cradle and quantiser examples; the model has room for them without inventing artificial time offsets.
- The hysteresis device of Example 1.7 (switching between two regimes with separated switching sets) is a template for describing which resource is the bottleneck either side of roughly 8 workers without chattering between regimes in the model.
- The impulsive-equation discussion (1.4.3) suggests modelling fixed-schedule events with count-down timers rather than exact time sets, if the model is later used for robustness arguments.

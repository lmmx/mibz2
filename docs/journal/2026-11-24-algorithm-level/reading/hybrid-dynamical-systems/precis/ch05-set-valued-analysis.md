# Chapter 5: Preliminaries from set-valued analysis (pp. 97–115)

The chapter supplies the set-valued analysis needed to talk rigorously about limits of hybrid arcs and about perturbing the flow and jump sets. It introduces convergence of sets (inner and outer limits), set-valued mappings with outer semicontinuity and local boundedness, graphical convergence, and the specialisation of these ideas to hybrid arcs, where graphical convergence and $(\tau,\varepsilon)$-closeness replace uniform convergence because jump times move. It closes with basic facts on constrained differential inclusions: viability, and the fact that limits of solutions under shrinking inflations of the data are still solutions. The material is selective, aimed at Chapter 6's well-posedness results, and most background proofs are omitted.

## 5.1 Set convergence (p. 97)

- **Definition 5.1** (p. 97). For sets $S_i\subset\mathbb R^n$: the *inner limit* $\liminf S_i$ is the set of points that are limits of some sequence $x_i\in S_i$ (one point for every $i$); the *outer limit* $\limsup S_i$ is the set of points that are limits of $x_k\in S_{i_k}$ along some subsequence. The sequence converges when the two agree. For singletons, the outer limit is the set of accumulation points and the inner limit is nonempty exactly when the points converge.
- Illustrations (p. 98): balls $r_i\mathbb B$ have inner and outer limits given by $\liminf r_i$ and $\limsup r_i$; "rotating rays" $\{x_2=a_ix_1\}$ converge when $a_i$ converges or diverges to $\pm\infty$ (to the vertical axis in the latter case); graphs of $t\mapsto t^i$ on $[0,1]$ converge to an L-shaped set made of the bottom edge and the right edge of the unit square.
- **Example 5.2** (p. 98–99). For $\dot z=f(z)$ with $f$ continuous and unique solutions, and $K$ compact, let $S_\tau$ be the set reachable from $K$ at time exactly $\tau$. If $\tau_i\to\tau$ then $S_\tau\subset\liminf S_{\tau_i}$ (by extending solutions slightly). If in addition every maximal solution from $K$ is complete, then $\limsup S_{\tau_i}\subset S_\tau$ by continuous dependence, so $S_{\tau_i}\to S_\tau$.
- Basic properties (p. 99): inner, outer limits and limits are closed; a sequence either "escapes to the horizon" (eventually misses every compact set) or has a nonempty outer limit; a monotone sequence that does not escape to the horizon converges.
- **Example 5.3** (p. 99). A convergent sequence of hybrid time domains $E_i$ converges to a hybrid time domain $E$, and $\mathrm{length}(E)=\lim\mathrm{length}(E_i)$. Convergence is equivalent to convergence of each slice $E_i\cap(\mathbb R_{\ge0}\times\{J\})$; slices of the limit are closed intervals, consecutive in the right way because right endpoints of slice $J$ equal left endpoints of slice $J+1$. The length is lower semicontinuous from the inner-limit property and upper semicontinuous because no points far beyond $\mathrm{length}(E)$ can persist.
- Hausdorff distance (p. 99–100): the infimum of $d$ with each set inside the $d$-inflation of the other. For rotating rays it is always infinite, yet the sets converge; uniformity appears only after truncation to balls.
- **Theorem 5.4** (p. 100). For closed $S$: (a) $S\subset\liminf S_i$ iff for all $r,\varepsilon>0$, eventually $S\cap r\mathbb B\subset S_i+\varepsilon\mathbb B$; (b) $S\supset\limsup S_i$ iff for all $r,\varepsilon>0$, eventually $S_i\cap r\mathbb B\subset S+\varepsilon\mathbb B$. Convergence is both together. For uniformly bounded sequences, set convergence is equivalent to Hausdorff distance tending to zero.
- **Example 5.5** (p. 100). Let continuous $\rho_i\ge 0$ tend to $0$ locally uniformly (for example $\rho_i=\delta_i\rho$) and $S$ be closed. The inflated sets $S_i=\{x: (x+\rho_i(x)\mathbb B)\cap S\neq\emptyset\}$ (5.1) are closed and converge to $S$. This is the model for sets seen through state-dependent measurement error, used heavily later.
- Linear images (p. 100): set convergence need not commute with linear maps (projection of rays converging to the horizontal axis onto the vertical axis gives the whole axis each time, but a point in the limit); the same can happen for bounded but not uniformly bounded sets.
- **Lemma 5.6** (p. 101). For a uniformly bounded convergent sequence and a linear $A$, $\lim AS_i=A\lim S_i$.
- **Theorem 5.7** (p. 101). Compactness: every sequence of sets either escapes to the horizon or has a subsequence converging to a nonempty set (a Bolzano–Weierstrass analogue).

## 5.2 Set-valued mappings (p. 101)

- **Definition 5.8** (p. 101). Range $\mathrm{rge}\,M$ and graph $\mathrm{gph}\,M$ of $M:\mathbb R^m\rightrightarrows\mathbb R^n$. A mapping defined on a subset $S$ is extended by the empty set outside $S$, and all notions are taken for that extension. A mapping is determined by its graph; it may be empty-, single- or multi-valued at a point.
- **Definition 5.9** (p. 102). *Outer semicontinuity* (osc) at $x$: whenever $x_i\to x$ and $y_i\in M(x_i)$ converge to $y$, then $y\in M(x)$. Equivalently, $\limsup M(x_i)\subset M(x)$ for every $x_i\to x$. Osc *relative to* $S$ is osc of the mapping restricted to $S$ (empty outside), checked at points of $S$. Inner semicontinuity is deliberately not used.
- Remarks (p. 102): a function is osc where it is continuous; but a single-valued osc mapping need not be continuous ($M(x)=0$ for $x\le0$, $1/x$ for $x>0$ is osc at $0$); local boundedness restores continuity. Osc implies closed values, not conversely.
- **Lemma 5.10** (p. 102). $M$ is osc iff its graph is closed; relative to $S$, iff the graph over $S$ is relatively closed in $S\times\mathbb R^n$.
- Illustration (p. 102–103, Figure 5.3): the sign-type step function is not osc; adding both values at the jump makes it osc; filling in the whole interval at the jump makes it osc and convex-valued. Likewise, whether a mapping that jumps from $\{0\}$ to $[-1,1]$ is osc depends on which side owns the boundary point.
- **Example 5.11** (p. 103). The argmin mapping $x\mapsto\{y\in K:\phi(x,y)=\min_K\phi(x,\cdot)\}$, with $\phi$ continuous and $K$ nonempty compact, is osc with nonempty values; convex values if $\phi(x,\cdot)$ is convex and $K$ convex.
- **Definition 5.12** (p. 103). The (Bouligand, contingent) *tangent cone* $T_S(x)$: all limits of difference quotients $(x_i-x)/\tau_i$ with $x_i\in S$, $x_i\to x$, $\tau_i\downarrow 0$. Nonempty, closed and containing a nonzero vector at non-isolated points of $S$; empty off $\overline S$; the tangent space for smooth surfaces. Figure 5.4 shows that at interior points it is all of $\mathbb R^n$ and that a flow map may or may not meet it at boundary points.
- **Example 5.13** (p. 104–105). Tangent cones of an interval (half-lines at endpoints, whole line inside; **erratum (p. 104):** for $S = [a, b]$, $T_S(a) = [0, \infty)$ and $T_S(b) = (-\infty, 0]$); of boxes (coordinatewise products); of the region under $\sqrt{x_1}$ at the origin (the right half-plane) and the region between $0$ and $\sin x_1$ at the origin (a wedge $w_1\ge0$, $w_2\le w_1$, presumably with $w_2\ge 0$ too; see parsing note). The interval case shows $T_S$ is generally not osc relative to $S$.
- **Definition 5.14** (p. 105). *Local boundedness* at $x$: some neighbourhood has bounded image; relative to $S$ as before.
- **Lemma 5.15** (p. 105). Osc versus upper semicontinuity (the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ condition $M(x')\subset M(x)+\varepsilon\mathbb B$ for $x'$ near $x$). If $M(x)$ is closed, upper semicontinuity at $x$ implies osc at $x$; if $M$ is locally bounded at $x$, osc implies upper semicontinuity (proof via Theorem 5.4(b)).
- **Lemma 5.16** (p. 105–106). For any $M$, the mappings $M_1(x)=\bigcap_{\delta>0}\overline{M(x+\delta\mathbb B)}$ and $M_2(x)=\bigcap_{\delta>0}\overline{\mathrm{con}}\,M(x+\delta\mathbb B)$ are osc, and locally bounded if $M$ is. These are exactly the Krasovskii regularisations of Definition 4.13.
- **Lemma 5.17** (p. 106). Inflations. For continuous $\rho\ge0$ and $M$ osc and locally bounded, $M_1(x)=M(x+\rho(x)\mathbb B)$ and $M_2(x)=\overline{\mathrm{con}}\,M(x+\rho(x)\mathbb B)$ are osc and locally bounded; when $m=n$, the output inflation $M_3(x)=\bigcup_{u\in M(x)}(u+\rho(u)\mathbb B)$ is too. (Proof by compactness and subsequence extraction, using Carathéodory for $M_2$.)
- **Definition 5.18** (p. 106–107). *Graphical convergence* of mappings: convergence of their graphs as sets; the graphical limit is the mapping with the limit graph. Example (p. 107): $|x|^i$ converges graphically to $0$ on $(-1,1)$, to $[0,\infty)$ at $\pm1$, and to the empty set elsewhere, unlike its pointwise limit. A constant sequence equal to a discontinuous function converges graphically to the closure of its graph.
- **Example 5.19** (p. 107). For graphically convergent $M_i$ with limit $M$: $\mathrm{dom}\,M\subset\lim\mathrm{dom}\,M_i$ and $\mathrm{rge}\,M\subset\lim\mathrm{rge}\,M_i$ (in the inner-limit sense). Under *local eventual boundedness* (for each compact $K$, eventually $M_i(K)$ lies in a fixed compact set) the domains converge: $\mathrm{dom}\,M=\lim\mathrm{dom}\,M_i$ (5.2).

## 5.3 Graphical convergence of hybrid arcs (p. 107)

- **Definition 5.20** (p. 108). The graph of a hybrid arc is the set of triples $(t,j,\varphi(t,j))$ in $\mathbb R^{n+2}$.
- **Definition 5.21** (p. 108). Hybrid arcs converge graphically if their graphs converge as sets; the limit is a set-valued map from $\mathbb R^2$. Equivalently, for each $j$ the slices $\varphi_i(\cdot,j)$ converge graphically.
- **Example 5.22** (p. 108). On $\mathbb R$ with $C=[0,9]$, $D=\{4\}$, $F(x)=2\sqrt x$ and $G(4)=1$, solutions from $1+\varepsilon$ flow as $(t+\sqrt{1+\varepsilon})^2$ until reaching $4$ at $t_1=2-\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}$, jump to $1$, and flow for two more time units. These are not the only maximal solutions (one may skip the jump since $4\in C$, or jump repeatedly; uniqueness also fails from $0$). The solutions from $1+\varepsilon$ and from $1$ are shown to be $\varepsilon$-close for $\varepsilon>0$ by explicit time shifts of $\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}-1\le\varepsilon$.
- Motivation (p. 109): for unbounded domains $\varepsilon$-closeness is too strict (two exponentials $\xi_ie^t$ are never uniformly close), so a truncated notion is needed.
- **Definition 5.23** (p. 109). *$(\tau,\varepsilon)$-closeness*: the two-sided matching of Definition 4.11 required only for points with $t+j\le\tau$. A variant, *$(T,J,\varepsilon)$-closeness*, uses $t\le T$, $j\le J$ (p. 109). The two are interchangeable for convergence purposes: "for every $\tau,\varepsilon$ eventually $(\tau,\varepsilon)$-close" is equivalent to "for every $T,J,\varepsilon$ eventually $(T,J,\varepsilon)$-close" (p. 110).
- **Definition 5.24** (p. 110). A sequence of hybrid arcs is *locally eventually bounded* if for every $m>0$ there are $i_0$ and a compact $K$ with $\varphi_i(t,j)\in K$ whenever $i>i_0$ and $t+j<m$.
- **Theorem 5.25** (p. 110). If for every $\tau\ge0,\varepsilon>0$ the arcs $\varphi_i$ are eventually $(\tau,\varepsilon)$-close to $\varphi$, then $\varphi_i\to\varphi$ graphically. Conversely, graphical convergence implies this eventual closeness when the sequence is locally eventually bounded. (Proof: $(r,\varepsilon/\sqrt2)$-closeness gives the truncated inclusions of Theorem 5.4.) Hence the solutions of Example 5.22 converge graphically as $\varepsilon\to0$.
- Contrast with classical convergence (p. 110–111): if the arcs of Example 5.22 are flattened into right-continuous functions of $t$ alone, the approximants from above converge pointwise to the right-continuous limit, but those from below converge to a left-continuous function differing at the jump time, so no pointwise limit exists for $\varepsilon\to0$; and even from above the convergence is not uniform (the gap at $t=1$ tends to $3$). Graphical convergence handles both sides uniformly.

## 5.4 Differential inclusions (p. 111)

- **Assumption $(\star)$** (p. 111). $C$ closed; $F$ osc and locally bounded relative to $C$; $F(x)$ nonempty and convex on $C$. Covers continuous $f$ on closed $C$. Constrained inclusion (5.4): $\dot z\in F(z)$, $z\in C$ a.e. on an interval.
- **Lemma 5.26** (p. 111). Viability, under $(\star)$. (a) Necessary: if a solution exists on $[0,T]$, $T>0$, then $F(z(0))\cap T_C(z(0))\neq\emptyset$ (immediate from the tangent cone definition when $\dot z(0)$ exists). (b) Sufficient: if $F(x)\cap T_C(x)\neq\emptyset$ for all $x\in C$ near $\xi$, a nontrivial solution starts at $\xi$. Both are cited from Aubin.
- **Assumption $(\star\star)$** (p. 112). $\rho$ continuous; $\delta_i\in(0,1)$ decreasing to $0$; each $z_i$ is a locally absolutely continuous solution, on an interval $I_i$, of the inflated system with $C_i=\{x:(x+\delta_i\rho(x)\mathbb B)\cap C\neq\emptyset\}$ and $F_i(x)=\overline{\mathrm{con}}\,F((x+\delta_i\rho(x)\mathbb B)\cap C)+\delta_i\rho(x)\mathbb B$ (5.5). These are precisely the flow parts of the perturbed system of Definition 6.27.
- **Lemma 5.27** (p. 112). Under $(\star)$ and $(\star\star)$, if all $I_i=[a,b]$ and $z_i\to z$ uniformly, then $z$ is absolutely continuous and solves (5.4). (Cited from Clarke; the classical version extracts a uniformly convergent subsequence, which here is done via Theorem 5.7 instead.)
- **Lemma 5.28** (p. 112). Under $(\star)$, $(\star\star)$, if $z_i$ are locally eventually bounded, converge graphically, the $I_i$ are closed on the left, and their left endpoints converge to $a$, then the graphical limit $z$ has domain $\lim I_i$, is single-valued and locally Lipschitz, and $z_i\to z$ uniformly on compact subsets of the interior of its domain. (Key step: eventual uniform Lipschitz bounds from the local boundedness of $F_1$ by Lemma 5.17.)
- **Theorem 5.29** (p. 113). Under the same hypotheses on convergence: (a) if locally eventually bounded, the graphical limit is a locally absolutely continuous solution of (5.4) on $\lim I_i$; (b) if the initial values converge but the sequence is not uniformly bounded (as printed; ChatGPT's review shows this literal hypothesis cannot be right: a sequence that is globally unbounded but locally eventually bounded, e.g. $z_i(t) = t$, has no finite-time blow-up. The coherent reading is *not locally eventually bounded*; check the page and errata), there is a smallest $t^*>a$ at which the arcs blow up along every sequence of times tending to $t^*$, and the graphical limit on $[a,t^*)$ is a locally absolutely continuous solution that is unbounded as $t\to t^*$.
- **Example 5.30** (p. 114). Reachable sets of the constrained inclusion at time $\tau$ from compact $K$: under $(\star)$ and the viability condition of Lemma 5.26(b) at every point of $C$, $S_\tau\subset\liminf S_{\tau_i}$; if also every maximal solution from $K$ is complete (automatic for compact $C$), then $\limsup S_{\tau_i}\subset S_\tau$, so the reachable set depends continuously on $\tau$ (using Theorem 5.7 and Theorem 5.29(a)).

## 5.5 Notes (p. 115)

Terminology follows Rockafellar and Wets, with Aubin–Frankowska, Aubin, Aubin–Cellina and Clarke as further references. Precise correspondences: Definition 5.1, Theorem 5.4, Theorem 5.7, Definitions 5.9, 5.12, 5.14, 5.18 and Lemma 5.15 come from Rockafellar–Wets; Lemma 5.26 from Aubin; Lemma 5.27 adapts a theorem of Clarke to this form of perturbation.

## Key ideas to carry away

- Sets converge via inner and outer limits; this is weaker than Hausdorff convergence but agrees with it on bounded regions after truncation (Theorem 5.4).
- Compactness is free: any sequence of sets either escapes to infinity or has a convergent subsequence (Theorem 5.7). This is what lets later chapters extract limits of solution sequences.
- Outer semicontinuity equals closed graph (Lemma 5.10); with local boundedness it equals upper semicontinuity (Lemma 5.15). These are the right regularity notions for flow and jump maps.
- Closing and convexifying a map over shrinking neighbourhoods, or over a continuous inflation radius, preserves osc and local boundedness (Lemmas 5.16, 5.17). This underwrites both the Krasovskii regularisation and the $\rho$-perturbed systems.
- For hybrid arcs, graphical convergence (with local eventual boundedness) is equivalent to eventual $(\tau,\varepsilon)$-closeness, which allows jump times to shift slightly (Theorem 5.25). Classical pointwise or uniform convergence fails near jumps.
- Limits of solutions of slightly inflated constrained inclusions are solutions of the original inclusion, or blow up at a well-defined finite time (Theorem 5.29).

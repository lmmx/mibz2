# Complementary bottlenecks: reading the ideal-parts matrix

Making the parts of mibz2's decoder free in turn and in combination shows where new algorithms would pay off and which two stages are worth far more together.
{: .post-dek }

{: .post-meta }

A bzip2 file is decoded by undoing, block by block, a sorting of the text called the
Burrows–Wheeler transform. For most of its history that undoing has been a walk: one row of the
sorted table at a time, each step a lookup that depends on the one before. This month mibz2 found
[a different way back](2026-11-10-rauzy-veech-reconstruction.md). Adjacent rows that hold the same
byte take the same step, so a whole stretch of them can be moved at once, and the classical tool for
doing that, the induction that mathematicians use to study interval exchanges, rebuilds the text
from a few retained rows and the words between them. It was a mathematical invention, not a tuning,
and it raised a question that tuning cannot answer: where in the decoder could another invention of
that kind make a real difference?

That question is harder than it sounds, because a decoder is not a list of costs. mibz2 decodes
on many cores at once: one thread scans each compressed block's Huffman codes, a pool of workers
rebuilds the blocks' text in parallel, and a reader hands the blocks back in order. The stages
overlap, they share caches and memory, and they hold one another back. A stage that takes a large
share of the running time may be worth little to improve, because another stage would take over as
the limit; a stage that looks cheap may be worth a great deal once something else is relieved.
Which algorithm is worth inventing therefore depends on the whole system, and we treated it as a
system: in the spirit of systems biology, which studies a living system as a network of interacting
parts through quantitative models fitted to measurement, and with the experimental method of Raj
Jain's course on [computer systems performance analysis](https://www.cs.wustl.edu/~jain/iucee/index.html),
which teaches how to design such interventions so that a few runs reveal how many factors combine.

This post follows that work from its motivation to its result. It describes the decoder as a
system of interacting stages and the levers by which such a system can be reorganised; then the
experiment that measured the decoder's counterfactual limits, by replacing each stage's work in turn,
and in combination, with answers recorded in advance; then what that experiment found, including a
pair of stages whose value appears only together and a residue it could not remove; and finally how
the results aim the mathematical search, through the principles the programme works with and four
concrete algorithms, each with its forecast and the test that would reject it.

**Takeaways**

- Below the knee, where adding a worker still speeds the decode up, building the inversion's
  structures (construction) is the largest opportunity: supplying it saves 42% of a one-worker
  decode and 25% at six workers.
- At eight workers the scanner and construction are complementary: supplying either alone saves
  3–6%, supplying both saves 36% (195 → 125 ms). An improvement measured on its own can look
  worthless because the system cannot yet use it.
- The experiment could not remove everything it meant to: the stand-in for emission still expanded
  every word into bytes, so the decoder's true floor is still to be measured.
- New mathematics can make a part cheaper by doing less work (contraction) or by making existing
  work yield more (enrichment). Four concrete algorithms follow from the measurements; each comes with
  a forecast and a test that would reject it, and none is yet measured.

## 1. Where could another mathematical breakthrough matter?

The programme behind this post set itself a large aim: not another few percent, but a decoder that
undoes the block sort with fundamentally less work
([its statement](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/essence.md)). The interval-exchange kernel
showed that such things exist. It came from a change of viewpoint, from following one row at a time
to moving whole stretches of rows that behave alike, and nothing in a profile of the old walk would
have suggested it. A second invention of that kind will not be found by measuring either. But
measurement can say where one would pay, and so where to look, and that is the purpose of
everything in this post.

The programme laid out the path as a chain of steps
([the programme](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/programme.md)). The first fixes the ambition and
its referee: what counts as a cheaper decoder, in which currency. The second characterises the
system: how computation, concurrency and memory combine into elapsed time. The third is a
counterfactual experiment: what would be gained if particular work became cheap. The fourth reads
the results as mathematical targets: which representations, dependencies or repeated computations
are expensive enough to be worth replacing. The last is invention itself: finding a computation that
captures a measured opportunity and can actually be built. The first two steps are the subject of
[Posing the inversion problem](2026-11-24-posing-the-inversion-problem.md) and
[The decoder as a queueing network](2026-11-25-the-decoder-as-a-queueing-network.md); this post is
about the third and fourth, and the beginning of the fifth.

Three questions about a decoder's speed are easily confused, and each needs a different experiment.

| Question | What answers it | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Where does the time go? | profiling, activity timings | how much work there is, and where |
| What limits the decode now? | scaling runs, and slowing one activity at a time | which constraints bind in the present configuration |
| What would be worth making cheaper? | making parts free, alone and together | the room an improvement could have, including room hidden behind another bottleneck |

The second and third differ because the decoder overlaps its work, so slowing a stage and speeding
it up are not mirror images. If the scanner and the workers could each get through a file in about
185 ms, slowing either would slow the whole decode, yet speeding either would leave the other still
taking 185 ms. The [earlier experiments](2026-11-25-the-decoder-as-a-queueing-network.md#11-testing-the-explanation-by-intervention)
answered the second question. Choosing what to invent needs the third.

The scanner (S) reads each block's coding tables and decodes its
[Huffman symbols](2026-11-05-huffman-bit-buffer.md) on a thread of its own. A worker then takes the
block through four stages. The move-to-front and second run-length stages (M) turn the symbols back
into the block's last column. Construction (C) builds the structures for undoing the block sort: the
retained rows, the interval-exchange reductions between them, the words those reductions spell and
the links that join them. Emission (E) follows those structures and expands the words into bytes, and
the final run-length expansion and checksum (R) finish the block. The reader (D)
delivers finished blocks to the caller in order, and as it takes each one it returns an admission
slot that lets the scanner start another, so the pipeline regulates itself through a feedback loop.

Described this way, the decoder is a *hybrid dynamical system*: between events, each thread makes
progress at a rate set by its work and by contention for the shared caches and memory; at events,
blocks are admitted, taken by a worker, finished and handed over, and each event changes which
threads can proceed. The description earns its keep because it lists exactly what a redesign can
change. Every proposal, whether a faster loop or a new piece of mathematics, alters one or more of
the system's ingredients, and naming which tells you what risks come with it
([the levers](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/reading/hybrid-dynamical-systems/relevance/levers.md)).

| What can change | Example in mibz2 | What to watch |
|---|---|---|
| **Flow rates:** how fast each activity runs | a faster Huffman decode; a cheaper induction | speeding one rate moves the bottleneck elsewhere |
| **Where work may proceed,** and where a thread must wait | the admission window; the in-order reader | thin, open conditions are fragile under perturbation |
| **When events may happen** | when a worker may take a block; when the scanner may admit one | the admission guard is an exact test, and widening it breaks the window |
| **What an event does** | which worker takes a block; how a block is routed | a choice within the rules is a policy, judged by a guarantee, not by one trace |
| **Who owns the information** | the buffers between scanner, workers and reader | every allocation needs an owner, and the earliest unfinished block must still get resources |
| **The state space itself** | interval-exchange induction in place of the row walk | a new representation must be compared with the old one by the same yardstick |

The last row is the one this post cares most about. The first five reorganise an existing
computation: they make it faster, move it, or schedule it differently. The last changes what the
computation consists of. The interval-exchange kernel was such a change, and the search this post
serves is for another. The experiment described next works mostly through the first lever, by
pushing chosen demands to zero, but its results can motivate a change to any of them, and the
proposals in §8 pull several at once.

The queueing view of the same system gives the vocabulary for its limits. The scanner is a single
server; the workers form a pool; the decode's throughput is bounded by whichever saturates first.
From one to four workers that bound predicted the *knee*, the worker count past which adding workers
stops helping, at 7.49 workers; the measured knee was 7.67
([the bottleneck law](2026-11-25-the-decoder-as-a-queueing-network.md#5-the-bottleneck-law)). Eight
workers, just past it, is now mibz2's default on the test machine. Below the knee the workers set the
pace; above it the scanner does.

## 3. Measuring a counterfactual

Profiling measures what the decoder does. To learn what it *would* do if some part became cheap, the
part has to be made cheap. For each of the six parts we built an *oracle*: a stand-in that hands the
decoder that part's correct output, recorded in an earlier run, so that the part's own computation
is skipped while everything downstream proceeds as usual. Each part then has two settings. *Real*
runs the ordinary computation; *ideal* replaces it with the oracle. When a later stage is made ideal
the earlier ones still run, so that the effect of each part can be attributed to it alone.

<aside class="il-gloss" markdown>**An oracle is not free.** Copying a recorded answer into the decoder still takes time and memory bandwidth. The experiment timed each oracle's handover in its own ledger entry, so its cost could be read beside the result, and §6 returns to what that cost hid.</aside>

Six parts with two settings each give $2^6 = 64$ configurations. Making each part ideal on its own
would have answered only half the question. The central issue is whether parts *interact*: whether
construction becomes more worth improving once the scanner has been improved. An interaction is a
property of combinations, and the method for measuring it is the *factorial design*, the core of
Jain's experimental method and the subject of the
[companion notes' tenth section](2026-11-25-the-decoder-as-a-queueing-network-notes.md#10-experimental-design-and-the-ideal-parts-matrix).
Running all 64 at each of four worker counts would have taken nearly half as long again as the
design used, and a well-chosen half answers almost everything the whole would.

The half is chosen by a rule called the *defining relation*, here $I = +SECMRD$. Code each part as
$-1$ when real and $+1$ when ideal; the rule keeps the configurations in which the product of all six
codes is $+1$, which are exactly those with an even number of parts ideal: none, two, four or all six.
That gives 32 configurations, and they have a useful property. Any two parts' effects, and any two
pairs' interactions, can be separated, because in the kept half no main effect or two-part
interaction is confounded with another. What is confounded is less likely to matter. Each main effect
shares its estimate with a five-part interaction, each two-part interaction with a four-part one, and
the three-part interactions are confounded with one another in pairs: the interaction of the scanner,
emission and construction cannot be told apart from that of the other three parts. A design with this
property is said to have *resolution VI*.

The half fraction has a gap that matters for the question at hand. It contains no configuration
with exactly one part ideal, so it can estimate each part's average effect across many settings of
the others, but it never directly measures the simplest counterfactual: today's decoder with one part
made free. Those two quantities are different, and §4 shows how different. So, before any data was
taken, the twelve configurations with one part ideal or one part real were added, making 44 at each
worker count ([the registration, clarification 8](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/ideal-parts/registration.md)). The fraction
estimates the effects and interactions; the added arms give each part's *room*, the decode time with
that part alone ideal divided by the time with every part real.

The worker counts were chosen to straddle the knee: one worker, which exposes nearly all of the
workers' computation; six, below it; eight, at the balance point; and ten, above it. Every
configuration ran the same binary, in randomised order within twelve blocks of rounds, with the
scanner pinned to one core and a 100 ms spin on every core before each decode to keep the processor's
clock from dropping ([the design](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/ideal-parts/design.md)). Every configuration also held every
oracle's recorded answers in memory, the all-real baseline included, so that all of them ran with the
same 495 MiB of extra data resident. The run took 2,492 decodes over four and a quarter hours. Every
output was byte-identical to the shipped decoder's, the scanner's pin held in all 352 traced decodes,
and the computations an oracle was meant to remove kept at most 0.19% of their usual busy time
([the results](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/ideal-parts/results/full/README.md)). The findings belong to that setting: an
Intel i9-10900K, a repeated Wikidata workload, and those stores, which mean that even the all-real
configuration ran in a different memory environment from an ordinary decode.

The registered rule labelled each part's room before the data arrived
([the rule](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/ideal-parts/registration.md)). A part has ROOM at a worker count if the upper end
of its 95% interval is at or below 0.95, so that making it free saves at least 5% with confidence;
NO ROOM if its whole interval lies within 2% of no change; and UNRESOLVED otherwise. Each room is a
geometric mean of paired ratios over the twelve blocks.

Several things are plain at once. Construction's opportunity is large below the knee and shrinks as
workers are added: 42.1% of a one-worker decode, 24.6% at six, but only 5.6% at eight. The scanner's
moves the other way, from nothing at one worker to 8.1% at ten, the only ROOM label above the knee.
Emission holds a substantial opportunity below the knee, 16.5% and 14.9%, and the table understates
it, for the reason §6 explains. The move-to-front and second run-length stages are smaller but real,
7.5% at one worker and 8.4% at six, and they sit directly upstream of construction. The final
run-length expansion and delivery show little room of their own. The last stages are not where the
next large gain will come from.

The same matrix can be read a second way, and the two readings are easily confused. The factorial
analysis of the 32 fraction configurations divides the variation in the logarithm of the decode time
among the parts and their interactions, and on that reading construction "explains" 80% at one worker
([the report](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/ideal-parts/results/full/report.txt)). That is 80% of the variation across the
experiment's configurations, not 80% of the running time. The activity timings put construction at
578 ms of a 1,283 ms one-worker decode, about 45%
([stage 1's report](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/math-targets/data/character-stage1/report.txt)). The distinction matters
most above the knee. At eight workers the scanner accounts for 46% of the variation, yet supplying it
alone saves only 3.2%: in most of the fraction's configurations several workers' parts are already
ideal, the workers have become cheap, and the scanner is exposed. Variation measures a part's average
importance across many hypothetical decoders; room measures its value in today's.

<aside class="il-gloss" markdown>**UNRESOLVED is not "no effect".** Construction at eight workers saves 5.6%, with an interval of 4.4–6.9%. It misses the ROOM label only because the rule asked for a whole interval beyond 5%; it is a real, modest saving.</aside>

What the table cannot show is how the parts depend on one another. At eight workers every part's
room is small, and a reader of this table alone would conclude that the decoder has little left to
give there. Two-part interactions carry about a fifth of the variation from six to ten workers, and
one of them changes that conclusion entirely.

## 5. Complementary bottlenecks

The interaction that dominates the experiment is between the scanner and construction. Its four
configurations, as times rather than ratios:

| Workers | All real (ms) | Construction supplied (ms) | Scanner supplied (ms) | Both supplied (ms) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1,306 | 756 | 1,319 | 751 |
| 6 | 243 | 183 | 237 | 143 |
| 8 | 195 | 184 | 189 | 125 |
| 10 | 196 | 186 | 180 | 149 |

At eight workers, supplying the scanner saves 6 ms and supplying construction saves 11 ms. Supplying
both saves 70 ms, taking the decode from 195 ms to 125 ms: 1.56 times the throughput. The
two parts are *complementary bottlenecks*: each holds the decode back as soon as the other is
relieved, so neither shows its value alone. On the logarithmic scale used by the registered analysis,
their interaction is $-0.110$ at six workers, with an interval of $[-0.112, -0.108]$, and $-0.071$ at
eight, carrying 16.9% and 13.1% of the variation; at one worker it is $-0.002$, indistinguishable from
nothing.

The interaction changes what a discovery is worth. Suppose a mathematical idea made construction
free. Measured in today's decoder at eight workers, it would save 5.6%, a result easily filed as
disappointing. Measured in a decoder whose scanner had been relieved, the same idea would save 33.9%
of the time that remained. The idea is the same in both cases; the system around it is not.

**A disappointing whole-decoder benchmark is therefore not evidence that an algorithmic improvement
lacks value.** It may be evidence that the system cannot yet exploit it. The converse is a research
direction in its own right: mathematical ideas that produced excellent local results and little
whole-decoder gain may deserve another look once the scanner has been restructured. Nor is the
scanner–construction pair the only route. Supplying the scanner together with emission's oracle saves
about 16% at eight workers with construction still real, and a change of representation could relieve
several parts at once ([the results, §5](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/ideal-parts/results/full/README.md)). What the
experiment establishes is that at eight workers relieving the scanner and construction together
exposes a far larger opportunity than either alone; it does not establish that no other combination
could.

A simple model makes the pattern intuitive. Suppose the scanner must do $S$ milliseconds of work on a
file, and the workers $W$ milliseconds between them, shared among $k$ workers. If the two stages
overlap perfectly, the elapsed time is set by whichever is slower:

$$
T \approx \max\left(S,\ \frac{W}{k}\right).
$$

If a change removes a fraction $a$ of the scanner's work and $b$ of the workers', the time becomes
$\max\big(S(1-a),\ W(1-b)/k\big)$. When the two are balanced, as they nearly are at eight workers, the
reduction is simply the smaller of $a$ and $b$: removing scanner work alone gains nothing until the
workers' share is removed too, and the other way round.

The model reproduces the shape of the measurement: the single changes gain almost nothing and the
pair gains a great deal. It overstates the pair, predicting a 45% saving where 36% was measured, and
that gap is informative. The model treats the scanner and the workers as independent stations, but
they share the processor's caches and memory bandwidth, so reducing the workers' computation also
changes how fast the scanner runs. The same coupling explains a result the model cannot: with the
scanner and construction both supplied, going from eight workers to ten *slows* the decode, from
125 ms to 149 ms. A revised model in the design analysis adds memory as a third station beside the
scanner and the workers, estimating that active workers slow the pinned scanner by about 6% through
contention and that memory binds once the parts are made fast
([the design analysis, §§1 and 6](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/7fab624eb204c591b0a72c925cf90bbad401396a/research/neon-movement/IDEAL-PARTS-2.md)). It remains a hypothesis: this experiment cannot separate the
decoder's own contention from the traffic of the oracles' stores.

## 6. What the experiment could not remove

The configuration with every part ideal should reveal what remains of the decoder when all of its
work has been supplied: its floor. It took 391 ms at one worker, 94 ms at six, 112 ms at eight and
142 ms at ten ([the results, §4](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/ideal-parts/results/full/README.md)). Those are not floors, for
two reasons, and the first concerns emission.

Emission does two kinds of work. It decides which word comes next, by following the structure that
construction built, and it expands each word into bytes. Its oracle supplies the words in output
order, which removes the first kind of work but not the second. The decoder's activity ledger,
however, charged the delivery of the supplied words and the expansion that remained to a single
entry, and the experiment's first write-up read that entry as the oracle's copying cost. The design
analysis, a second pass over the results, found the mistake by reading the oracle's code and divided
the entry using earlier measurements of the same workload
([the design analysis, §3.1](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/7fab624eb204c591b0a72c925cf90bbad401396a/research/neon-movement/IDEAL-PARTS-2.md)); an independent review then verified the division and drew its
consequences ([the review, §4](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/ideal-parts/reviews/2026-11-26-chatgpt.md)).

| Inside emission's oracle entry, at one worker | Time (ms) |
|---|---:|
| delivering the supplied words | 25.8 |
| expanding the words into bytes | 259.4 |
| together | 285.2 |

About nine tenths of the entry is real decoding work. So emission's 16.5% is not a ceiling on what
improving emission could save, and the configuration with every part nominally ideal still did most
of emission's work.

The second reason is the oracles' own cost. At one worker the workers' handovers, emission's word
expansion included, account for 377 ms of the all-ideal configuration's 391 ms. At eight and ten workers, many threads copy large recorded buffers at once, and
the all-ideal wall *rises* from 112 ms to 142 ms while each thread's handover barely changes, which is
what contention for memory bandwidth would produce. Neither effect is the decoder's.

A short follow-up run is designed to separate the pieces
([the floor run](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/pull/917)). Its oracles hand over their answers
without copying, and it runs two floors: one in which the words are still expanded, and one in which
the expansion is removed as well, leaving only the cost of the pipeline itself: its queues, locks and
thread hand-offs. The design analysis estimates that second residue by arithmetic at about 14 ms at
one worker and roughly 12 to 19 ms at six to ten workers, 14–17 ms at eight
([the design analysis, §3.1](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/7fab624eb204c591b0a72c925cf90bbad401396a/research/neon-movement/IDEAL-PARTS-2.md)). That is a forecast for the run to test, not a measurement, and it
is not a decompression time: a real decoder must still compute the answers the oracles supply. The
run's purpose is narrow, to keep experimental copying and leftover emission work from being mistaken
for a limit of the decoder, and it leaves the scanner–construction result untouched.

## 7. From measured opportunity to mathematical invention

The experiment says where time could be saved. It does not say how. A part can be made cheaper by
doing its present computation faster, and that is engineering; or by finding a different computation
with the same result, and that is where mathematics enters. The programme works with two principles
for finding such computations, and a third idea that constrains them both
([the programme's statement](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/essence.md);
[What else moves together](2026-11-24-what-else-moves-together.md)).

### Contraction: changing the unit of computation

The interval-exchange kernel rests on one observation. When the decoder undoes the block sort, each
row of the sorted table maps to another by a rule called LF, and adjacent rows holding the same byte
map to adjacent rows. So a run of equal bytes can be moved as one interval rather than row by row, and
an induction that repeatedly composes these interval moves, and recurses on what is left, rebuilds the
text from far fewer steps than there are rows.

This is *contraction along composition*: recognise that several computations are the same, perform
it once, and repeat on what remains. Its two ingredients are a notion of "these move together",
here adjacency with an equal byte, and a rule for composing what moves together. The question for
further invention is whether a different notion exists: rows that move together up to a consistent
relabelling, a repeated stretch of the *text* rather than of the rows, a recurrence among the
induction's own steps, or, in the programme's broadest phrasing, transformations that are secretly
the same computation expressed in different coordinates.

### Closure, and the scope of the negative results

Any contraction must survive its own success. After two computations are composed, the result must
still be cheap to use: the description of what to read, the lookup of each row's successor and the
words to be written must all stay compact. That property is *closure*, and without it the work has
merely been deferred.

Two negative results bound the search, and both are narrower than they first appear. The first, the
floor called G1, says that rows with no neighbour holding the same byte have nothing to move together
with in the sense above; but it is a floor on operations charged in a calculus of rigid interval moves,
not a proved lower bound on the decoder's memory reads, and the bridge between the two is unproved
([Posing the inversion problem, §2](2026-11-24-posing-the-inversion-problem.md#2-steps-1-and-2-the-referee-and-the-toy-problem)).
The second answers the closure problem negatively within one class: a conservation law shows that
composing the retained-row map keeps the form of its access schedule but not its compactness on
high-entropy rows, which explains why one round of composition left the access pattern 21 to 24 times
more fragmented ([the journal, §§42 and 48](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level.md)). Neither result rules out a different
representation. They rule out squeezing harder on the same notion of moving together, and that is
why the search looks outside the existing family.

### Enrichment: making existing work yield more

The second principle came from automatic differentiation. A program that computes a function can be
run over a richer algebra, the *dual numbers*, in which every value carries its derivative alongside
it, and multiplication follows the product rule:

$$
(a, a')\,(b, b') = (ab,\ a'b + ab').
$$

The ordinary computation proceeds unchanged, and the extra information comes out with it at small
cost. In an earlier project of the maintainer's, exact gradients obtained this way made the optimiser
of a library that flattens photographed pages about eleven times faster on single images
([page-dewarp](https://github.com/lmmx/page-dewarp)), not by doing the same work faster but by making
a better algorithm possible. *Enrichment along composition* asks the same of the decoder: what extra
quantity could ride along an existing step at little cost, and would it make a later stage cheap or
unnecessary? A sharper form of the question is which later operation is necessary only because an
earlier stage failed to keep, or cannot cheaply use, information it already met.

There are four natural sites. The Huffman decode sees every symbol of a block before anything else
does; the move-to-front stage reconstructs every byte of the last column; the induction composes the
interval moves; construction builds the retained graph that emission walks. Each could carry a rider.
The test that separates a real rider from a restatement is strict: it must be cheaply acquired, and it
must not simply be the answer in disguise. Carrying each row's position in the text, for example,
fails, because that position is what the decode computes.

Some limits are already known. The final LF targets cannot be computed before a block's last byte is
seen: changing that byte shifts the targets of between 15% and 50% of the rows on every Wikidata block
tested, so no part of the induction or the wiring can move ahead of the block's end into the scanner
([the design analysis, §4](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/7fab624eb204c591b0a72c925cf90bbad401396a/research/neon-movement/IDEAL-PARTS-2.md)). That is a limit on acquiring the final answer early. It is not an
impossibility result for a summary gathered during the scan and finished cheaply once the block ends.

### The cost of a candidate, in four currencies

A candidate is judged by real machine cost, counted in four currencies with the cost of building
every structure included
([the formulation](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/reports/2026-11-24-from-search-to-formulated-problem.md)):

- $Q$, the unpredictable memory reads, the addressed reads whose location depends on data;
- $D$, the depth of dependent access, the longest chain of reads that must wait on one another;
- $P$, the passes over the data, at most two;
- $S$, the working state held at once.

The currencies keep a candidate honest. An idea that removes reads by adding a pass, or shortens a
chain by holding far more state, has moved its cost rather than removed it, and only the accounting
across all four shows which. A large reduction in $D$ is a real result; the programme's stated
breakthrough is a reduction in $Q$, fewer than one unpredictable read per row with no same-byte
neighbour. The rules referee candidates once they exist. They do not generate them.

### Four kinds of number

The work uses numbers of four different kinds, and they must not be read as competing estimates of
one quantity.

| Kind | What it establishes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Lower bound** | under stated assumptions, a computation cannot cost less | any order of the induction's steps takes at least 1.220 steps for each run of the original column |
| **Oracle measurement** | an observed counterfactual, with particular work supplied | 125 ms at eight workers with the scanner and construction supplied |
| **Predictor** | an estimate of what a concrete proposed design would cost | 130–145 ms for a pooled scan with cheaper construction; 14–17 ms for the floor run's residue |
| **Upper bound** | under stated assumptions, completion takes no longer | none proved yet for this decoder |

Lower bounds prune the space of designs, oracle measurements say where the opportunity is, predictors
rank the candidates that might capture it, and upper bounds, when they exist, guarantee a result.

### A. The induction schedule

The induction is the part of construction most open to mathematics, and its cost has an exact count.
For every order in which its steps can be taken,

$$
\text{steps} = r_2 - 1 + U,
$$

where $r_2$ counts the distinct pairs of adjacent run letters, so that $r_2 - 1$ is a floor no order can
beat, and $U$ counts the extra boundaries that a particular order creates along the way
([the induction record, §5.6](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/monodromy/2026-11-10-rauzy-veech.md)).
On all 233 Wikidata blocks, the historical order, which always takes the step at the right end, needs
1.812 steps for each run of the original column. An order that takes first the steps that create no
new boundary, and otherwise the largest cell, needs 1.279, and the floor is 1.220. These counts were taken
on the earlier full exchange over all run letters, not on the object the present kernel builds.

The better order cuts the steps by $1 - 1.279/1.812 \approx 29.4\%$. If the whole of the induction
sub-phase, 216 ms at one worker, scaled with the step count, that would save $216 \times 0.294 \approx
63.5$ ms, 4.9% of the 1,306 ms decode. It will not all scale: setup and the dictionary of words do not
depend on the order. Writing $f$ for the share of the sub-phase whose cost does scale with the number of
steps, and $c$ for the new order's own cost,

$$
\text{net saving} \approx 216\ \text{ms} \times f \times 29.4\% - c .
$$

The programme's gate for pursuing an idea is 3% of the decode, 39.2 ms at one worker.

The fold removes the intermediate representation where the fast path allows it: the move-to-front
loop emits each maximal run once, and construction accumulates it directly into the counts and queues
its later phases need. Functional programmers call this
[*deforestation*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deforestation_(computer_science)), eliminating the intermediate
data structure between two transformations. The capped runs add 3,990 to 5,284 records a block,
3.5–10.2% of the runs. Removing them saves one pass and about 360 MB of reads and writes over the
workload, and 180 KB of state per worker. In the four currencies, $Q$ and $D$ are unchanged and $P$
and $S$ fall. The forecast is 110–145 ms at one worker, about 8.5–11% of the workers' time. The risk is
branch misprediction in the move-to-front loop, and the rejection test is a ten-minute profile that
splits construction's first pass: if pushing rows into the queues dominates it and the capping and
rejoining take under 50 ms, the fold is dropped in favour of the wiring ([the design analysis, §5](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/7fab624eb204c591b0a72c925cf90bbad401396a/research/neon-movement/IDEAL-PARTS-2.md)).

### C. Forward wiring with lanes

Construction's last sub-phase wires the retained rows into a cycle, and emission walks that cycle to
write the text. Today each link is stored as the XOR of its two neighbours, $\text{link}(i) =
\text{prev}(i) \oplus \text{next}(i)$, which saves memory and lets emission walk from one mark in both
directions at once. But a step along an XOR link must know where it came from, so each walk is a chain
of dependent reads, and wiring the links costs some 106 million scattered read-modify-writes on the
Wikidata workload.

The proposal stores the successor alone, $\text{link}(i) = \text{next}(i)$, which can be written
sequentially, and walks the cycle forward only, in several lanes at once. The cycle is cut at $K$ anchors
chosen uniformly at random; each lane walks one arc from its anchor to the next, and a lane that
finishes takes the next unvisited anchor. The arcs come out in the wrong order, so a final pass stitches
them together and performs the run-length expansion and checksum segment by segment.

There is real mathematics in choosing $K$. Uniformly placed anchors cut a cycle of length $m$ into
uneven arcs, and the walk is not finished until its longest arc is. In the continuous-spacing model the
expected longest of $K$ arcs is

$$
\mathbb{E}[L_{\max}] \approx m\,\frac{H_K}{K},
$$

where $H_K = 1 + \tfrac12 + \dots + \tfrac1K$ is the $K$-th harmonic number. With as many anchors as
lanes, eight of each, the longest arc averages $0.340\,m$, nearly three times an even share, and the
eight lanes behave like three. With four times as many anchors, $K = 32$, it falls to $0.127\,m$, almost
exactly the even share of $m/8$. That is why the design takes many more anchors than lanes.

The attraction is that one change relieves several costs. Construction's wiring becomes sequential;
emission gains several independent chains of reads to overlap, which the design analysis estimates
lifts the reads in flight from about two to between six and seven; the backward expansion of words
disappears; and the final pass can consume the segments directly. The forecast is 280–340 ms at one
worker, 21–26% of the workers' time, at the price of 1.0–1.5 MB of scratch per worker. In the four
currencies, $D$ falls by about a factor of three while $Q$ is unchanged. The rejection test is a
standalone harness on the 932 blocks of the workload that the kernel decodes: if the new path does not save at least 190 ms at one
worker, it is dropped for a simpler prefetch on the present links ([the design analysis, §5](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/7fab624eb204c591b0a72c925cf90bbad401396a/research/neon-movement/IDEAL-PARTS-2.md)).

This proposal crosses a stage boundary, and it illustrates the experiment's lesson in miniature: a
different consumer allows a cheaper structure to be built. It also illustrates the currencies. A walk
in several lanes can make the decoder much faster without reducing the number of unpredictable reads,
and if it does, the research question about reads remains open. Both outcomes deserve to be recorded
as what they are.

### D. The pooled scan

The scanner oracle deleted the Huffman work from the decode. A pooled scan would not delete it; it
would change where it is done, which is a different lever and a different question. Today one thread
decodes every block's Huffman codes in turn and hands the symbols to the workers through a queue.
Pooled, a fast pass first finds where each block starts, by searching every bit alignment of the file
for the 48-bit marker that bzip2 places at the head of each block, and each worker then scans its own
block before rebuilding it.

This is why the project is not simply looking for a faster Huffman routine: it is asking whether
Huffman decoding needs to be a serial station at all. The two-station model of §5 shows why the answer
interacts with construction. If a block costs $s$ on the scanner and $w$ on a worker, a serial scanner
caps the throughput at $1/s$ blocks per unit time however many workers there are, while $k$ pooled
workers, each doing both jobs, complete about $k/(s + w)$. The knee puts $w$ at about $7.5\,s$, so at
eight or nine workers, where the two stations are nearly balanced, pooling alone gains little in this
model. As construction gets cheaper, though, $w$ shrinks and the pooled rate rises, while the serial cap
does not move. The pooled scan and cheaper construction are the
complementary pair of §5, made into designs.

The design analysis estimates the marker search at 1–2 ms on the 47 MB file, with a false match
probability around one in a million per file, and the removal of a symbols queue carrying about 400 MB
of traffic. It forecasts 172–175 ms at eight or nine pooled workers on its own, 10–12% below today's
195 ms, and 130–145 ms combined with cheaper construction. The measured 125 ms with both parts supplied
makes that a sensible scale to aim at, though it does not validate the forecast. The rejection tests
are a five-minute timing of the marker search, which fails above 10 ms, and a minimal prototype, which
fails unless it beats 195 ms by at least 7% at eight or nine workers within a bounded memory budget. The
cheaper route of enriching the serial scanner with construction's work is closed: it would slow the
station already on the critical path, as a fused scanner tried earlier did, and the final LF targets
cannot be known before a block ends ([the design analysis, §§4–5](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/7fab624eb204c591b0a72c925cf90bbad401396a/research/neon-movement/IDEAL-PARTS-2.md)).

### The four, side by side

| Proposal | Changes | Forecast saving | Extra cost | Rejected if |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Induction schedule** | the order of the induction's steps | at most 63.5 ms at one worker; gate 39.2 ms | the schedule's own bookkeeping | step share × cut < 3% of the decode |
| **B. Direct fold** | MTF and construction's first pass | 110–145 ms at one worker | branch mispredictions in the MTF loop | queue pushes dominate the first pass, and capping and rejoining take < 50 ms |
| **C. Forward wiring with lanes** | wiring, emission, final expansion | 280–340 ms at one worker | 1.0–1.5 MB scratch a worker; stitching the arcs | saves < 190 ms at one worker |
| **D. Pooled scan** | where the Huffman scan runs | 172–175 ms at eight workers; 130–145 ms with cheaper construction | the marker search; a compressed look-ahead window | marker search > 10 ms, or < 7% faster than 195 ms |

All four are forecasts by one analysis, with different degrees of support behind them, and the
one-worker figures say nothing directly about eight workers, where the scanner limits. What the
experiment adds is a way to read them together: B and C reduce the workers' demand and so lower the
knee, D removes the serial limit above it, and the largest prize lies in combining a worker-side
reduction with the pooled scan.

## 9. From characterisation to construction

The experiment set out to say where another mathematical invention would pay, and it has. Below the
knee, construction and emission hold most of the opportunity, with the move-to-front stages a real
third. Above it, the scanner does, and at eight workers the scanner and construction form a
complementary pair whose joint value is many times what either shows alone. The last stages hold
little. The decoder's true floor is still unknown, because the experiment's emission oracle left the
word expansion in place.

Each measurement that remains has a single decision to make, and then it stops
([the plan](https://github.com/lmmx/mibz2/blob/37ffea9e5980f2685053a730f7e7c9014217776f/docs/journal/2026-11-24-algorithm-level/plan.md)).

| Question | Why its answer changes a decision |
|---|---|
| What remains once the oracles' copying and the word expansion are removed? | whether the pipeline's own overhead deserves work, and what the decoder's floor really is |
| How much of the induction sub-phase scales with the number of steps? | whether a better induction schedule can pass its gate |
| Does a proposed design win across the whole path it touches? | whether to develop that implementation further |

The first is the zero-copy floor run, nine configurations at one, six, seven, eight, nine and ten
workers, a focused follow-up rather than a second matrix. The second is the induction census. The third
is each design's own rejection test, and the designs for the pooled scan, the direct fold and forward
wiring can begin now, alongside the two closing measurements.

What the experiment makes possible is a different kind of search. Before it, a mathematical idea was
judged by whether it made one part of the decoder faster, and an idea that did so without moving the
whole decode looked like a failure. Now each candidate can be aimed at a measured opportunity, priced
against the part it changes, and paired with whatever companion change lets that saving reach the
output. The matrix was the means. The subject is how a scientific understanding of a computational
system can direct the search for cheaper algorithms, and what that search should look for next: not
only faster stages, but representations in which the stages' work is shared, avoided, or never needed
at all.
